# Founder Canon: The YC School of Early-Stage Startup Pragmatism

Research pack, part 1 of the startup-wisdom doc set.

**Purpose.** Primary-source research for an agent skill that snaps coding/analysis
agents OUT of a proof-and-rigor-maximizing mindset and INTO an early-stage
startup mindset: a 3-person, seed-funded team that makes decisions at 50-70%
information, treats most decisions as reversible, and optimizes for learning
speed and usefulness over defensibility.

**Method.** Every source below was fetched and read in full (paulgraham.com
essays, michaelseibel.com posts, YC Startup Library transcripts, Sam Altman's
blog and Startup Playbook, First Round Review, Reid Hoffman's own writing,
podcast transcripts). All verbatim quotes were string-verified against the
fetched source text.

---

## 1. Paul Graham

### 1.1 "Do Things That Don't Scale"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/ds.html (July 2013)

Distilled principles (actionable form):

- Do not wait for takeoff; cause it. Recruit users one at a time, by hand,
  even when the per-user effort looks absurd on a spreadsheet.
- Judge early efforts by growth rate, not absolute numbers. Manual,
  unscalable effort compounds: a few hand-recruited users at 10% weekly
  growth beat a big launch that goes nowhere.
- Deliberately narrow the market to a pool you can dominate fast (the
  Facebook-at-Harvard move). Small + winnable now beats big + unprovable.
- Over-serve early users to an "engine turning over" degree: make the
  *experience* of being an early user insanely great even while the product
  itself is primitive and embarrassing.
- Treat manual work as a legitimate substitute for software. Do the thing by
  hand first (Stripe installing integrations for users, DoorDash-style
  concierge ops); automate only what survives contact with users.
- Launch to get users, not to make a splash. Big launches are a distraction;
  a trickle of real users teaches more than a wave of press.
- Use the "founders make it take off" fact as a diagnostic: if you are
  waiting for something external (data, perfection, permission) before
  acting, you are doing it wrong.

Verbatim quotes:

> "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to
> recruit users manually. Nearly all startups have to." — Paul Graham

> "Startups take off because the founders make them take off." — Paul Graham

> "The question to ask about an early stage startup is not 'is this company
> taking over the world?' but 'how big could this company get if the founders
> did the right things?'" — Paul Graham

> "You should take extraordinary measures not just to acquire users, but
> also to make them happy." — Paul Graham

> "It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of
> being your user." — Paul Graham

### 1.2 "How to Start a Startup"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/start.html (March 2005)

Distilled principles:

- The whole game is three things: good people, something customers actually
  want, and spending as little money as possible. Every other activity is
  overhead.
- Assume your initial plan is wrong; your first job is to find out *where*
  it is wrong, which only contact with users can reveal.
- Get a prototype in front of users and refine from their reactions —
  "Version 1.0" thinking loses to iterate-in-production thinking.
- Ideas are worth little by themselves; execution by adaptable people is the
  asset. Do not defend the idea; evolve it.
- Frugality is a strategy, not an austerity measure: low burn = more
  iterations = more chances to find what works.

Verbatim quotes:

> "You need three things to create a successful startup: to start with good
> people, to make something customers actually want, and to spend as little
> money as possible." — Paul Graham

> "In a startup, your initial plans are almost certain to be wrong in some
> way, and your first priority should be to figure out where." — Paul Graham

> "The only way to make something customers want is to get a prototype in
> front of them and refine it based on their reactions." — Paul Graham

> "What matters is not ideas, but the people who have them. Good people can
> fix bad ideas, but good ideas can't save bad people." — Paul Graham

> "Build something users love, and spend less than you make." — Paul Graham

### 1.3 "Startup = Growth"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/growth.html (September 2012)

Distilled principles:

- Define yourself by growth rate and nothing else. Pick a weekly growth
  target and let it make every prioritization decision for you.
- Use growth as an evolutionary pressure on the idea itself: whatever you
  must change to keep hitting 10%/week is the real product discovering
  itself.
- A weekly cadence forces decision speed: you cannot run a six-week analysis
  when the metric resets every seven days.
- Optimizing for growth converts vague strategic debates ("should we do X?")
  into a single empirical question: does it get us our target this week?
- Benchmark: 5-7% a week is good; 10% a week is exceptional; 1% means you
  have not figured out what you are doing yet.

Verbatim quotes:

> "A startup is a company designed to grow fast." — Paul Graham

> "If you want to understand startups, understand growth. Growth drives
> everything in this world." — Paul Graham

> "We usually advise startups to pick a growth rate they think they can hit,
> and then just try to hit it every week." — Paul Graham

> "A good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week. If you can hit 10% a week
> you're doing exceptionally well. If you can only manage 1%, it's a sign you
> haven't yet figured out what you're doing." — Paul Graham

### 1.4 "The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html (October 2006)

Distilled principles:

- There is really only ONE fatal mistake: not making something users want.
  All 18 named mistakes are just mechanisms for committing it.
- Slowness in launching is a canonical killer. Launching forces you to
  finish some quantum of work and confronts you with reality; excuses for
  delay are usually fear dressed as diligence.
- The mirror-image mistake, launching too early, is rare and less deadly:
  the risk is ruining your reputation with a broken v1, but the bar is only
  "some subset of users are helped."
- Raising too much money and hiring too fast slow you down and remove the
  pressure that forces learning.
- Effort spent on a problem is not defensible value. Users do not reward the
  work you did; they reward the problem you solved for them.

Verbatim quotes:

> "In a sense there's just one mistake that kills startups: not making
> something users want. If you make something users want, you'll probably be
> fine, whatever else you do or don't do." — Paul Graham

> "Startups make all kinds of excuses for delaying their launch. Most are
> equivalent to the ones people use for procrastinating in everyday life."
> — Paul Graham

> "One reason to launch quickly is that it forces you to actually finish
> some quantum of work." — Paul Graham

> "The early adopters you need to impress are fairly tolerant. They don't
> expect a newly launched product to do everything; it just has to do
> *something*." — Paul Graham

### 1.5 "Ramen Profitable"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/ramenprofitable.html (July 2009)

Distilled principles:

- Aim for the smallest revenue level that removes existential dependency:
  founders' living costs covered = infinite runway = time becomes your ally.
- Ramen profitability is a speed tool, not a lifestyle: it buys you the
  right to keep iterating without permission from investors.
- Getting to any revenue quickly forces the discipline of charging real
  users real money for real value — the strongest possible signal loop.
- It changes your negotiating position with everyone (investors, acquirers,
  yourself): you can say no, so you decide faster and with less fear.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Ramen profitable means a startup makes just enough to pay the founders'
> living expenses." — Paul Graham

> "Now survival is the default, instead of dying." — Paul Graham

> "When people start to pay you significant amounts, the company starts to
> feel real. And your own living expenses are the milestone you feel most,
> because at that point the future flips state." — Paul Graham

### 1.6 "Default Alive or Default Dead?"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/aord.html (October 2015)

Distilled principles:

- Always know one number: at current expenses and growth, do you make it to
  profitability on the money you have? If you cannot answer instantly, stop
  and compute it.
- Have the "default dead" conversation early — it is nearly free to worry
  too early and often fatal to worry too late.
- Never build a plan whose success step is "and then investors save us."
  Say the dependency out loud; if it sounds alarming, it is.
- The fatal pinch = default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it.
  Every week of slow deciding moves you closer to it.
- Overhiring is the most common way promising startups kill themselves;
  headcount is not progress.

Verbatim quotes:

> "The fatal pinch is default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix
> it." — Paul Graham

> "Say 'we're default dead, but we're counting on investors to save us.'
> Maybe as you say that, it will set off the same alarms in your head that it
> does in mine." — Paul Graham

> "Half the founders I talk to don't know whether they're default alive or
> default dead." — Paul Graham

### 1.7 "Schlep Blindness"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/schlep.html (January 2012)

Distilled principles:

- The most valuable problems are the tedious, painful, unsexy ones —
  precisely because everyone else's unconscious filters them out before they
  are even considered.
- Do not ask "is this pleasant to build?"; ask "does someone desperately
  need this?" Pain of implementation is a moat, not a warning.
- Treat your own flinch away from grubby work (sales calls, integrations,
  regulatory schleps, manual data cleanup) as a signal you may be near value.
- Trick yourself if needed: commit to the problem first, discover the
  schleps later — that is how most successful founders actually did it.

Verbatim quotes:

> "The most dangerous thing about our dislike of schleps is that much of it
> is unconscious. Your unconscious won't even let you see ideas that involve
> painful schleps." — Paul Graham

> "The most striking example I know of schlep blindness is Stripe, or rather
> Stripe's idea. For over a decade, every hacker who'd ever had to process
> payments online knew how painful the experience was." — Paul Graham

> "In addition to their intrinsic value, they're like undervalued stocks in
> the sense that there's less demand for them among founders."
> — Paul Graham (on hard/annoying problems)

### 1.8 "How Not to Die"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/die.html (August 2007)

Distilled principles:

- Survival is the strategy: most startups that stay alive and keep shipping
  eventually reach something that works. Dying is almost always a choice
  (giving up), not an event.
- Keep making forward motion, however small. Momentum, not perfection, is
  what keeps a startup alive.
- Engineer commitment: make quitting publicly embarrassing so the default
  action is to keep going.
- Expect the desert: the period where the product is out, growth is slow,
  and everything feels theoretical. Plan to cross it, not to avoid it.

Verbatim quotes:

> "If you can just avoid dying, you get rich." — Paul Graham

> "Startups rarely die in mid keystroke. So keep typing!" — Paul Graham

> "Founders are more motivated by the fear of looking bad than by the hope
> of getting millions of dollars. So if you want to get millions of dollars,
> put yourself in a position where failure will be public and humiliating."
> — Paul Graham

### 1.9 "Relentlessly Resourceful"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/relres.html (March 2009)

Distilled principles:

- The two-word job description of a good founder: relentlessly resourceful.
  Not diligent, not rigorous — resourceful, and without stopping.
- When the world says no (a blocked API, a dead-end analysis, a channel that
  does not convert), immediately search for another route instead of
  documenting why the no was justified.
- "I could not proceed because X" is a failure of resourcefulness unless you
  tried at least one workaround for X.
- The opposite of relentlessly resourceful is "hapless" — passively
  accepting the constraints handed to you. Audit yourself for haplessness.

Verbatim quotes:

> "A couple days ago I finally got being a good startup founder down to two
> words: relentlessly resourceful." — Paul Graham

> "Not merely relentless. That's not enough to make things go your way
> except in a few mostly uninteresting domains. In any interesting domain,
> the difficulties will be novel. Which means you can't simply plow through
> them, because you don't know initially how hard they are; you don't know
> whether you're about to plow through a block of foam or granite."
> — Paul Graham

### 1.10 "Founder Mode"
URL: https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html (September 2024)

Distilled principles (Garry-Tan-adjacent; Tan is a credited draft reader):

- The professional-manager playbook ("hire good people and give them room")
  is often disastrous advice for founder-led companies; staying close to the
  details is a feature, not micromanagement.
- Skip-level engagement with the actual work is legitimate: the leader (or
  agent) should touch reality directly, not only manage abstractions and
  reports about reality.
- Conventional "best practice" is calibrated for large, slow organizations;
  applying it to a tiny fast one damages the tiny one.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Hire good people and give them room to do their jobs. Sounds great when
> it's described that way, doesn't it? Except in practice, judging from the
> report of founder after founder, what this often turns out to mean is: hire
> professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground."
> — Paul Graham

> "There are things founders can do that managers can't, and not doing them
> feels wrong to founders, because it is." — Paul Graham

## 2. Garry Tan

### 2.1 "Better, Faster, Cheaper — Being first doesn't matter"
URL: https://medium.com/initialized-capital/better-faster-cheaper-cf510c1fc32

Distilled principles:

- Being first is irrelevant; being better, faster, or cheaper *for the
  customer, right now* is what wins. Ship the version that is concretely
  faster/cheaper today rather than the defensible version that ships later.
- Speed of the product experience is itself a feature customers pay for
  (Uber vs taxis, Facebook vs Friendster); speed of the team is how you get
  there.
- Compare yourself against the user's current alternative (often paper,
  spreadsheets, or nothing), not against a theoretical perfect competitor.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Speed matters a lot, especially for consumer scenarios. There's a whole
> internet of other things to occupy someone's time, and if a particular
> website is slow, it matters." — Garry Tan

> "If you're using software, you're beating paper and pencil, and that, by
> default, makes things faster." — Garry Tan

### 2.2 On YC's core loop, launching first, and speed (Tetragrammaton
podcast with Rick Rubin, plus his blog)
URLs: https://podscripts.co/podcasts/tetragrammaton-with-rick-rubin/garry-tan ;
https://blog.garrytan.com/

Distilled principles:

- The entire filter is one question: will this person actually *make
  something people want*? Making comes before analyzing; "everyone can make
  something" now, so the differentiator is choosing what and shipping it.
- Competition should compress your timeline: knowing a rival will launch
  should make you launch first, not analyze harder.
- The observed magic of YC is that it "speeds founders up" — the same
  people, under deadline and peer pressure, ship in weeks what they
  otherwise would not ship in years. Environment and cadence create speed.
- Advice is compiled failure data: "there's about a thousand ways that
  startups fail, and we've seen every single one of them" — so borrow the
  pattern-matching instead of re-deriving it from first principles.
- Default-alive thinking applies at every scale (his Gobble/Ooshma video
  essay: "Sell? Die? No. Grow profitably... why default alive matters").

Verbatim quotes:

> "On the first day, you get a t-shirt that says, make something people
> want. And if you look at the 10 minutes, that's all we really care about.
> It's like, is this person going to make something?" — Garry Tan

> "I knew that they were going to launch, and we were going to, we needed to
> launch first." — Garry Tan (on his YC batch competitor)

> "I've never seen anything speed founders up in such a profound way."
> — Garry Tan (on YC's deadline-driven batch structure)

> "The dollars and cents come after you make something people want."
> — Garry Tan

### 2.3 "New Rules for Founders" (Startup School 2024 keynote)
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fsTtKywmWlU

Distilled principles:

- "Don't LARP": do not perform startup-building (decks, frameworks, process
  theater, elaborate validation) — actually build and put it in front of
  users. For agents: do not perform rigor; produce the useful artifact.
- Small teams with AI leverage can now out-ship large ones; the constraint
  is decision speed and taste, not headcount or infrastructure.
- Launch embarrassingly early and iterate in public; adoption follows
  shipped reality, not polished plans.

## 3. Michael Seibel

### 3.1 "How to Build an MVP" (YC Startup Library)
URL: https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp

Distilled principles:

- Launch something quickly and iterate; surveys, interviews, and competitor
  analysis are distractions dressed as diligence. "You only really start
  learning about your user when you put a product in front of them."
- Give the MVP a hard deadline measured in weeks. Write the spec down, then
  cut it: for each feature ask "does a truly desperate customer need this to
  start?"
- Build for hair-on-fire customers who will accept a brick, not a fire
  hose: desperate users adopt imperfect solutions, and they are the only
  users worth serving with v1.
- Do not fear a failed demo; the company does not die from one bad user
  interaction. Fear is real but acting on it (delaying launch) is the error.
- Even Steve Jobs iterated: the first iPhone had no App Store, no video, 2G.
  "Fake Steve Jobs" perfectionism is a misreading of how great products were
  actually built.
- Do not fall in love with the MVP; fall in love with the customer and the
  problem.

Verbatim quotes:

> "It is bad to spend one year building your MVP because you're afraid the
> first customer might not like it." — Michael Seibel

> "Your customers are experts in their problem, but they actually don't have
> all of the answers and how to solve their problem. That's your job."
> — Michael Seibel

> "The faster you learn, the more likely you are to build something that
> people love before anyone else." — Michael Seibel

> "It's far better to have a hundred people love your product than a hundred
> thousand who kind of like it." — Michael Seibel

> "Don't fall in love with your MVP. It's gonna change." — Michael Seibel

### 3.2 "Building Product" (YC Startup Library lecture)
URL: https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product

Distilled principles:

- Users define usefulness. A product (or an analysis) that users do not find
  useful is by definition not useful, no matter how well-crafted; "products
  are not paintings."
- Keep the MVP window at ~2 weeks; the longer the build, the more the
  product drifts from the actual problem and customer.
- Giving the product to customers is a *required step*, not an optional
  validation phase after internal quality gates.
- Constrain spending and ego: low burn bought Justin.tv "a lot of ability to
  screw up and make mistakes" — cheap iterations are the real safety net.
- Gut-check every piece against "does this solve the user's problem?" and
  accept that the honest answer usually hurts.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Products are not paint. They're not art. If users don't find products
> useful, then the products are by definition not useful, and they're a
> waste of your time to build." — Michael Seibel

> "The startup world is very unforgiving to artists." — Michael Seibel

> "If you decide to only build your MVP in two weeks, it's a lot easier to
> stay on task and make sure you're actually solving that problem for that
> customer." — Michael Seibel

### 3.3 "YC's Essential Startup Advice"
URL: https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/yc-s-essential-startup-advice
(with Geoff Ralston)

Distilled principles:

- Launch right away — a mediocre product now, containing a "quantum of
  utility," beats a perfect product later.
- Then do things that don't scale: get the first customers by any means
  necessary, including manual work that could never serve 100 customers.
- Apply Paul Buchheit's 90/10 rule to every feature list: find the version
  that delivers 90% of the value for 10% of the effort. There is almost
  always one.
- The only two tasks that matter pre-product-market-fit: write code and
  talk to users. Everything else (conferences, press, corp dev, process) is
  a distraction.
- Growth follows a good product; a good product comes from launch → talk →
  iterate loops, never from more upfront analysis.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Launching a mediocre product as soon as possible, and then talking to
> customers and iterating, is much better than waiting to build the
> 'perfect' product." — Michael Seibel & Geoff Ralston

> "Look for a way in which you can accomplish 90% of what you want with only
> 10% of the work/effort/time. If you search hard for it, there is almost
> always a 90/10 solution available." — Michael Seibel & Geoff Ralston
> (crediting Paul Buchheit)

> "A 90% solution to a real customer problem which is available right away,
> is much better than a 100% solution that takes ages to build."
> — Michael Seibel & Geoff Ralston

> "The most important tasks for an early stage company are to write code and
> talk to users." — Michael Seibel & Geoff Ralston

### 3.4 "The Real Product Market Fit"
URL: https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit

Distilled principles:

- Hold the problem tightly and the solution loosely. Your v1 solution idea
  is "usually wrong"; only launching and iterating finds the real one.
- Product-market fit is being overwhelmed by usage — not employee count,
  funding, or a defensible architecture. Until then: stay lean, keep burn
  low, small-team shape.
- Pick users whose problem is so dire they will accept a half-baked v1;
  their tolerance is your permission to ship early.
- "The company is the product" (Seibel's recurring frame, also in his MVP
  and product talks): as a founder your real product is the machine that
  learns and ships — the team, cadence, and decision loop — not any single
  artifact it emits. Optimize the loop, not the artifact.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Founders often hold too tightly onto solutions and too loosely onto
> problems. The problem, i.e. the market, is the real opportunity."
> — Michael Seibel

> "Your unique and special v1 idea on how to solve that problem is usually
> wrong and only through launching, talking to customers, and iterating will
> you actually find a product that reaches product market fit."
> — Michael Seibel

> "You need to find problems so dire that users are willing to try
> half-baked, v1, imperfect solutions." — Michael Seibel

> "Until then, stay lean, keep burn low, and resemble a Navy SEAL team
> instead of an Army battalion." — Michael Seibel

### 3.5 "Users don't care about your tech stack" (recurring Seibel/YC
product doctrine)
URLs: https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7y-how-to-build-a-product-with-reddit-s-steve-huffman-and-twitch-s-emmett-shear ;
https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product

Distilled principles:

- Users experience outcomes, not implementations. Time spent making the
  internals impressive is invisible to the person you are serving.
- Choose boring, fast tools you already know; the tech stack is a means to
  the iteration loop, never the deliverable.
- The corollary for analysis/engineering work: nobody downstream cares how
  rigorous the machinery was if the answer arrives too late to matter.

Verbatim quote (from the YC product talk with Huffman & Shear, same
doctrine):

> "Your users don't care. Uh, I'll repeat what I said before: your users
> don't care about your tech." — YC "How to build a product" session

## 4. Sam Altman

### 4.1 "Idea Generation"
URL: https://blog.samaltman.com/idea-generation

Distilled principles:

- Good ideas come from volume plus tolerance for sounding silly: surround
  yourself with (or simulate) an environment where half-formed ideas are
  voiced early instead of filtered for defensibility.
- The best ideas are fragile — a rigor-first filter kills exactly the ideas
  most worth testing. Protect weird ideas long enough to test them cheaply.
- Ask "why now?" and "what has recently become possible?" rather than "can
  I prove this will work?"

Verbatim quotes:

> "The best ideas are fragile; most people don't even start talking about
> them at all because they sound silly." — Sam Altman

> "It's important to be in the right kind of environment, and around the
> right kind of people." — Sam Altman

### 4.2 "Startup Playbook"
URL: https://playbook.samaltman.com/

Distilled principles:

- Love beats like: build something a small number of users love, then
  expand — never the reverse.
- Momentum is the prime directive of execution. Growth "solves all
  problems"; lack of growth is solvable only by growth. Never trade
  momentum for polish.
- Decisiveness under conflicting advice is a core founder skill: listen to
  everything, "then quickly make a decision and go with it" — even at 50-70%
  confidence.
- Responsiveness is a proxy for decision speed and intensity; slow replies
  and slow calls are a tell of a failing operation.
- Do a few things and do them intensely fast; "I have never, not once, seen
  a slow-moving founder be really successful."
- No exceptionalism about your domain: rockets and nuclear reactors move
  fast; your project is not the special case that justifies slowness.

Verbatim quotes:

> "It's much better to first make a product a small number of users love
> than a product that a large number of users like." — Sam Altman

> "Growth and momentum are the keys to great execution." — Sam Altman

> "The prime directive of great execution is 'never lose momentum'."
> — Sam Altman

> "Great founders listen to all of the advice and then quickly make their
> own decisions." — Sam Altman

> "While great founders don't do many big projects, they do whatever they do
> very intensely. They get things done very quickly." — Sam Altman

> "I have never, not once, seen a slow-moving founder be really successful."
> — Sam Altman

> "All failing companies have a pet explanation for why they are different
> and don't have to move fast." — Sam Altman

### 4.3 "How To Be Successful"
URL: https://blog.samaltman.com/how-to-be-successful

Distilled principles:

- Compound yourself: pick loops where each iteration makes the next one
  cheaper and faster. Learning rate is the exponent.
- Focus: working on the right thing dominates working many hours. Cut work
  that does not move the one metric that matters.
- Be willing to look wrong in the short term; consensus-seeking and
  defensibility-seeking are the same trap wearing different clothes.

Verbatim quotes:

> "It is much more important to work on the right thing than it is to work
> many hours." — Sam Altman

> "Compounding is magic. Look for it everywhere." — Sam Altman

> "You will usually get risk calculations wrong." — Sam Altman (on why
> people avoid non-consensus bets)

## 5. Jessica Livingston

### 5.1 "Jessica Livingston's Pretty Complete List on How Not to Fail"
URL: https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-not-to-fail/ (Female Founders
Conference keynote, 2016)

Distilled principles:

- Nothing else you do matters if you are not making something people want.
  Skill at any other activity cannot compensate.
- The early-stage loop is exactly two activities: build stuff and talk to
  users. "And nothing else." Everything beyond that pair is a
  wolf-in-sheep's-clothing distraction (partnerships, PR, conferences,
  advisory boards, arguing on the internet).
- Talk to users even when it doesn't scale; no startup has ever
  over-invested in talking to users.
- Hold the idea loosely: most good ideas evolve (Airbnb went from airbeds
  at conferences to whole homes because they said yes to an unplanned user
  request).
- Solve a problem you yourself have, then graduate to other people's
  version of it as fast as possible.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Nothing else you do will matter if you are not making something people
> want." — Jessica Livingston

> "Talk to your users as much as you can, even if that means doing things
> that don't scale early on. I don't know of a single case of a startup that
> felt they spent too much time talking to users." — Jessica Livingston

> "Your users are your guidepost. And the way you stay on the right path in
> the early stages of a startup is to build stuff and talk to users. And
> nothing else." — Jessica Livingston

> "Be open to adjusting your idea, because most good ideas evolve."
> — Jessica Livingston

### 5.2 "Think Different. Think Users." / "Grow the Puzzle Around You"
URLs: https://foundersatwork.posthaven.com/think-different-think-users ;
https://foundersatwork.posthaven.com/grow-the-puzzle-around-you

Distilled principles:

- Non-obvious, user-grounded conviction beats credentialed consensus; the
  earliest YC bet worked because it focused on users when no one else did.
- Start small so you can be nimble and open to change; smallness is an
  advantage in decision speed, not a deficiency to apologize for.
- Focus on making something people want; don't let rejection or status
  games distract you.

## 6. Dalton Caldwell

### 6.1 "Dalton & Michael: Should You Follow Your Passion?" (YC video)
URL: https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DW-dalton-michael-should-you-follow-your-passion

Distilled principles:

- Beware "solution in search of a problem": having built something clever is
  not evidence anyone needs it. Start from a problem holder, not from the
  artifact.
- If your "passion" is the same as everyone else's (consumer apps, social,
  music, coffee), it is not an edge; zoom out and pick by market reality
  instead of taste.
- Founding teams are rarely uniformly passionate about the problem —
  commitment to winning as a team substitutes fine. Passion is not a
  prerequisite for excellent execution.

Verbatim quotes:

> "Solution in search of a problem is where you build a thing and you're
> like, 'We have this thing.'" — Dalton Caldwell

> "To the extent that the stuff that you care about the most is the same as
> everyone else, or you don't actually have unique passions, you might want
> to zoom out a little bit and not have passion be your primary mode of
> making a decision." — Dalton Caldwell

### 6.2 Tarpit ideas + "Just don't die" (Lenny's Podcast interview;
YC video "Avoid These Tempting Startup Tarpit Ideas")
URLs: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/lessons-from-1000-yc-startups ;
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m7LvNTbaqSI

Distilled principles:

- A tarpit idea *feels* validated — it attracts positive feedback, seems
  like an unsolved problem, and has good arguments behind it — and that is
  exactly what makes it a trap. Positive feedback is not evidence; shipped
  usage is.
- Distinguish "hard" from "tarpit": a merely hard idea is fine; a tarpit is
  an idea whose apparent validation is the illusion.
- The core survival mantra is brutally simple: just don't die. Keep the
  team together, keep shipping, stay solvent — most companies fail by
  founders quitting, not by being out-competed.
- Simple pragmatic advice beats sophisticated strategy at the early stage:
  "sell shit, make money."
- A good pivot moves toward your expertise ("a good pivot is like going
  home"), and toward harder, less crowded terrain ("move towards the
  mountains and the desert") — away from the crowded oasis of ideas everyone
  has.

Verbatim quotes:

> "One of these themes is simple pragmatic advice. Sell shit, make money.
> One of my mantras is just don't die." — Dalton Caldwell

> "The weird aspect of what we call a tarpit idea is an idea that a lot of
> people come up with and then it seems like an unsolved problem and you get
> lots of positive feedback for." — Dalton Caldwell (transcript lightly
> normalized for transcription spelling)

> "A good pivot is like going home. It's warmer. It's closer to something
> that you're an expert at." — Dalton Caldwell

## 7. Paul Buchheit

### 7.1 "If your product is Great, it doesn't need to be Good"
URL: https://paulbuchheit.blogspot.com/2010/02/if-your-product-is-great-it-doesnt-need-to-be-good.html

Distilled principles:

- Pick three key attributes, get those very, very right, and forget
  everything else. Those three define the essence; the rest is noise.
- A first version that is great at its essence and bad at everything else
  beats a first version that is uniformly good: the iPod dominated despite
  being worse than rivals on most spec-sheet dimensions.
- Cutting scope is not lowering quality; it is *finding* the product. The
  forced trade-off reveals the true value proposition.
- Serve a few people's real need completely rather than many people's needs
  partially (the origin of the "100 people who love you" doctrine, which
  Buchheit seeded inside YC).
- This is also the source of the 90/10 rule YC teaches: deliver 90% of the
  value with 10% of the effort, now.

Verbatim quotes:

> "What's the right approach to new products? Pick three key attributes or
> features, get those things very, very right, and then forget about
> everything else." — Paul Buchheit

> "By focusing on only a few core features in the first version, you are
> forced to find the true essence and value of the product." — Paul Buchheit

> "If your product needs 'everything' in order to be good, then it's
> probably not very innovative (though it might be a nice upgrade to an
> existing product)." — Paul Buchheit

## 8. Reid Hoffman

### 8.1 The launch-too-late law
URL: https://medium.com/@reidhoffman/is-this-going-to-destroy-the-company-if-not-let-them-test-it-d6ead87d99a9
(Hoffman restating his own long-standing rule; popularized everywhere)

Distilled principles:

- If the first release does not embarrass you, you spent too long on it.
  Embarrassment at v1 is the *correct calibration point* for launch timing.
- Gate experiments with one question — "is this going to destroy the
  company?" — and if not, let them run. Map decisions by blast radius, not
  by completeness of justification.
- Speed of shipping compounds into learning advantage (the Facebook
  "10,000 versions at any given time" model he praises).

Verbatim quotes:

> "I've long believed that if you're not embarrassed by your first product
> release, you've released too late." — Reid Hoffman

> "Is this going to destroy the company? If not, let them test it."
> — Reid Hoffman (quoting the Zuckerberg decision rule he endorses)

## 9. Dave Girouard — "Speed as a Habit" (First Round Review)
URL: https://review.firstround.com/speed-as-a-habit/

(Adjacent canon: the standard operationalization of "speed" that YC-school
founders circulate.)

Distilled principles:

- All else being equal, the fastest company in any market wins. Speed is a
  habit you build deliberately, not a personality trait.
- Split every decision into *what* and *when*: decide when the decision will
  be made before debating what it should be. Most decisions deserve ten
  minutes, not days.
- Classify decisions by reversibility. Very few decisions cannot be undone;
  treat reversible ones with cheap, fast calls and reserve deliberation for
  the rare irreversible ones.
- A fast wrong decision usually beats a slow right one, because the fast one
  gets corrected while the slow one blocks everything behind it.
- Never accept the fast-vs-good tradeoff as an excuse; in most real cases it
  is a false shield used to justify losing momentum.

Verbatim quotes:

> "I've long believed that speed is the ultimate weapon in business. All
> else being equal, the fastest company in any market will win."
> — Dave Girouard

> "Fast decisions are far better than slow ones and radically better than no
> decisions." — Dave Girouard (on Upstart's operating belief)

> "There are decisions that deserve days of debate and analysis, but the
> vast majority aren't worth more than 10 minutes." — Dave Girouard

> "It's important to internalize how irreversible, fatal or non-fatal a
> decision may be. Very few can't be undone." — Dave Girouard

> "There's not always a stark tradeoff between something done fast and done
> well. Don't let you or your organization use that as a false shield or
> excuse to lose momentum." — Dave Girouard

## 10. Cross-Source Synthesis: 10 Load-Bearing Principles

Target mindset shift: **stop maximizing scientific defensibility, start
maximizing decision speed and learning.** Each principle below is stated as
an operating rule for a 3-person, seed-funded team (or an agent emulating
one), with its canonical sources.

### P1. Usefulness is defined by the user, not by the quality of the work
An artifact nobody uses is worthless regardless of its rigor. "Products are
not paint" (Seibel, Building Product); "just one mistake that kills
startups: not making something users want" (PG, 18 Mistakes); "Nothing else
you do will matter if you are not making something people want" (Livingston,
How Not to Fail). Rule: before polishing anything, name the user and the
decision it unblocks; if you can't, stop.

### P2. Ship at the point of embarrassment, then iterate
"If you're not embarrassed by your first product release, you've released
too late" (Hoffman); "launching a mediocre product as soon as possible ...
is much better than waiting" (Seibel/Ralston, YC Essential Advice); slowness
in launching is a canonical startup killer (PG, 18 Mistakes). Rule: define
the smallest artifact containing a "quantum of utility" and release it now;
embarrassment is calibration, not failure.

### P3. Take the 90/10 solution, always
"A 90% solution to a real customer problem which is available right away, is
much better than a 100% solution that takes ages to build" (Buchheit via
Seibel/Ralston); "pick three key attributes ... and then forget about
everything else" (Buchheit, Great vs Good). Rule: for every task, explicitly
search for the 10%-effort version before starting the 100% version; the
missing 10% of quality is usually noise, and the forced cut reveals the
essence.

### P4. Decide fast; match deliberation to reversibility, not to available
rigor
"Fast decisions are far better than slow ones and radically better than no
decisions"; "the vast majority aren't worth more than 10 minutes"; "very few
can't be undone" (Girouard, Speed as a Habit); "Is this going to destroy the
company? If not, let them test it" (Hoffman); "great founders listen to all
of the advice and then quickly make their own decisions" (Altman, Playbook).
Rule: classify each decision as reversible/irreversible first; reversible
ones get minutes and a default-yes to action at 50-70% information.

### P5. Learning rate is the only compounding asset — protect the loop
"The faster you learn, the more likely you are to build something that
people love before anyone else" (Seibel, MVP); "compounding is magic"
(Altman, How To Be Successful); pick a weekly growth/learning target and let
it drive every prioritization (PG, Startup = Growth). Rule: measure progress
in iterations-per-week and lessons-per-iteration, never in thoroughness of
any single iteration.

### P6. Contact with reality beats analysis — surveys and proofs are not
evidence
"You only really start learning about your user when you put a product in
front of them"; "your customers are experts in their problem, but they don't
have the answers" (Seibel, MVP); "your initial plans are almost certain to
be wrong ... your first priority should be to figure out where" (PG, How to
Start a Startup); tarpit ideas are exactly the ones with abundant positive
feedback and no shipped usage (Caldwell). Rule: treat internal validation
(more analysis, more review, more simulated proof) as procrastination once a
real-world test is available; positive feedback without usage counts as
zero.

### P7. Do things that don't scale — manual now beats automated later
Recruit users by hand; do the work manually; narrow the market until you can
win it this week (PG, Do Things That Don't Scale); get the first customers
"by any means necessary" (Seibel/Ralston); it always "starts personal"
(Tan). Rule: prefer the manual, unscalable, direct version of any process
until the thing it supports has proven demand; building scalable
infrastructure early is deferred-usefulness disguised as diligence.

### P8. Momentum is existential — know if you are default alive
"The prime directive of great execution is 'never lose momentum'" (Altman,
Playbook); know whether you are default alive or default dead, and never
plan on being rescued (PG, Default Alive); "just don't die" and keep the
team shipping (Caldwell); ramen profitability flips survival to the default
state (PG, Ramen Profitable). Rule: track runway (time, budget, attention)
explicitly; any week without shipped forward motion moves you toward the
fatal pinch; low burn buys iterations, and iterations are life.

### P9. Be relentlessly resourceful — a blocked path is a routing problem
"Relentlessly resourceful" is the entire founder job description in two
words (PG, RelRes); schlep blindness hides the highest-value work behind
tedium (PG, Schlep Blindness); startups rarely die in mid keystroke — "so
keep typing!" (PG, How Not to Die). Rule: "blocked" must always be
translated into "here are the two workarounds I tried and the third I am
trying"; the tedious, unglamorous path is often the valuable one precisely
because it repels everyone else.

### P10. Optimize the machine, not the artifact — the company is the product
Your real product is the learning loop itself: the team, cadence, and
decision speed that emit artifact after artifact (Seibel's recurring "the
company is the product" frame; Tan on YC's structure "speed[ing] founders up
in such a profound way"; PG's founder mode — stay close to the details, skip
the manager-mode process theater; "don't LARP" — build, don't perform
building). Rule: when tempted to perfect a single output, ask instead what
change to the loop (shorter deadline, tighter spec cut, earlier user
contact, faster decision default) would make every future output better —
and make that change instead.
