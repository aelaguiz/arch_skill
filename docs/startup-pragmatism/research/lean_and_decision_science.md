# Lean & Decision-Science School of Early-Stage Pragmatism

Research doc, part 2 of the startup-wisdom pack. Every section below is grounded in a
fetched primary source (title + URL given). Quotes are verbatim from the source text.

**Angle for downstream use:** this material powers an agent skill that reality-checks
agents out of proof/receipt/perfection-maximizing behavior and into 3-person seed-stage
startup mode: decide at 50-70% information, prefer reversible fast moves, and optimize
for learning per unit time. Each section ends with "agent translation" bullets where the
mapping is not obvious.

---

## 1. Eric Ries — The Lean Startup

**Source:** "The Lean Startup Methodology" — http://theleanstartup.com/principles
**Source:** "Why vanity metrics are dangerous" — http://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/12/why-vanity-metrics-are-dangerous.html

### Distilled principles

- A startup is "a human institution designed to create a new product or service under
  conditions of extreme uncertainty." Under extreme uncertainty, the classic management
  playbook (detailed plans, long polish cycles, full specs) is the wrong tool — but so is
  abandoning all process. The discipline that fits uncertainty is *running experiments*,
  not perfecting outputs.
- The unit of progress is **validated learning**, not features shipped, artifacts
  produced, or proof accumulated. If an activity does not test a hypothesis about what
  customers/users actually want, it is waste no matter how well-executed.
- **Build-Measure-Learn:** turn ideas into a minimum product, measure real response,
  learn whether to pivot or persevere. "All successful startup processes should be geared
  to accelerate that feedback loop." Speed of the loop — not thoroughness of any one pass
  through it — is what you optimize.
- The **MVP** exists "to begin the process of learning as quickly as possible," not to be
  a small-but-polished v1. Its quality bar is "can we learn from it," not "are we proud
  of every edge case."
- The killer failure mode is spending months "perfecting that product without ever
  showing the product, even in a very rudimentary form, to the prospective customer."
  Perfection before contact with reality is the classic death.
- **Innovation accounting** replaces vanity metrics with *actionable* metrics: numbers
  that demonstrate cause and effect, are per-person rather than gross totals, and tell
  you what to do next. A metric you can't act on is decoration.
- **Vanity metrics corrupt decision-making**, not just reporting: when numbers go up
  everyone credits their own work; when they go down everyone blames someone else, and
  each team drifts into "their own private reality."

### Verbatim quotes

> "Too many startups begin with an idea for a product that they think people want. They
> then spend months, sometimes years, perfecting that product without ever showing the
> product, even in a very rudimentary form, to the prospective customer."
> — theleanstartup.com/principles

> "The unit of progress for Lean Startups is validated learning-a rigorous method for
> demonstrating progress when one is embedded in the soil of extreme uncertainty."
> — theleanstartup.com/principles

> "Startups exist not to make stuff, make money, or serve customers. They exist to learn
> how to build a sustainable business." — theleanstartup.com/principles

> "When we rely on vanity metrics, a funny thing happens. When the numbers go up, I've
> personally witnessed everyone in the company naturally attributing that rise to
> whatever they were working on at the time. ... when the numbers go down, we invariably
> blame someone else. Over time, this allows each person in the company to live in their
> own private reality." — Eric Ries, "Why vanity metrics are dangerous"

### Agent translation

- "Months perfecting without showing anyone" = long autonomous runs that polish
  artifacts, proofs, and receipts before any human or reality contact. Ship the
  rudimentary version and learn.
- An agent's "validated learning" is: did the user/world confirm this was the right
  thing at all? Receipts that prove *you did the work correctly* are vanity metrics if
  the work itself was never validated as worth doing.

## 2. Steve Blank — Customer Development

**Source:** "Nail the Customer Development Manifesto to the Wall" —
https://steveblank.com/2012/03/29/nail-the-customer-development-manifesto/
**Source:** "No Business Plan Survives First Contact With A Customer — The 5.2 billion
dollar mistake" — https://steveblank.com/2010/11/01/no-business-plan-survives-first-contact-with-a-customer-%E2%80%93-the-5-2-billion-dollar-mistake/

### Distilled principles

- "A startup is a temporary organization designed to *search* for a repeatable and
  scalable business model." Search mode and execution mode are different jobs with
  different standards of rigor. Applying execution-grade rigor while still searching is
  a category error.
- Manifesto rule #1: **"There Are No Facts Inside Your Building, So Get Outside."**
  Analysis, planning, and internal debate cannot produce the facts that matter; only
  contact with reality can.
- Manifesto rule #8: **"No Business Plan Survives First Contact with Customers."**
  Plans are hypotheses. The Iridium case study: an eleven-year, $5.2B, technically
  flawless execution of a plan whose assumptions (1990 cell coverage, prices, handset
  sizes) were obsolete by launch. "It was a technical tour de force" — and a total loss.
- Manifesto rule #12: **"Fast, Fearless Decision-Making, Cycle Time, Speed and Tempo."**
  Speed is an explicit first-class principle of the search phase, on the same list as
  validating hypotheses with experiments.
- Manifesto rules #3-#4: failure is an integral part of the search; "If You're Afraid to
  Fail You're Destined to Do So." A process that punishes every wrong step teaches the
  searcher to stop searching.
- Blank's Iridium post-mortem: the fatal move was executing "a business plan frozen in
  time" — continuing to invest rigor and capital into assumptions that were never
  re-tested against the world as it changed.

### Verbatim quotes

> "There Are No Facts Inside Your Building, So Get Outside."
> — Customer Development Manifesto, rule 1

> "No Business Plan Survives First Contact with Customers."
> — Customer Development Manifesto, rule 8

> "Fast, Fearless Decision-Making, Cycle Time, Speed and Tempo."
> — Customer Development Manifesto, rule 12

> "Business plans are the leading cause of startup death."
> — Steve Blank, "Lessons Learned," Iridium post

> "Rapidly changing markets require continuous business model iteration/customer
> development." — Steve Blank, Iridium post

### Agent translation

- "Inside the building" = inside the agent's own reasoning, test harness, and artifact
  pile. Confidence built purely from internal analysis and self-generated proof is
  building-bound confidence; it must be discounted until it touches the user or
  production reality.
- Iridium is what maximal rigor without revalidation looks like: perfect execution of
  an unvalidated spec. An agent that flawlessly implements a stale interpretation of
  intent is running the Iridium play.

## 3. Jeff Bezos — One-Way Doors, 70% Information, High-Velocity Decisions

**Source:** Amazon 2015 Letter to Shareholders (published April 2016) —
https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF
**Source:** Amazon 2016 Letter to Shareholders ("Day 1", published April 2017) —
https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders

### Distilled principles

- **Type 1 vs Type 2 decisions.** Type 1: "consequential and irreversible or nearly
  irreversible — one-way doors — and these decisions must be made methodically,
  carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation." Type 2: "changeable,
  reversible — they're two-way doors ... Type 2 decisions can and should be made quickly
  by high judgment individuals or small groups." Sort the decision *before* choosing the
  process.
- **The one-size-fits-all trap**: "As organizations get larger, there seems to be a
  tendency to use the heavy-weight Type 1 decision-making process on most decisions,
  including many Type 2 decisions. The end result of this is slowness, unthoughtful
  risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently, and consequently diminished
  invention." (2015 letter.) The failure mode is not making bad decisions — it is
  applying Type-1 ceremony to Type-2 doors.
- **Decide at ~70% of the information you wish you had.** Waiting for 90% is "probably
  being slow." The compensating skill is not more analysis, it is being "good at quickly
  recognizing and correcting bad decisions."
- **Being wrong is cheaper than being slow — if you course-correct fast.** "If you're
  good at course correcting, being wrong may be less costly than you think, whereas
  being slow is going to be expensive for sure."
- **Disagree and commit** converts unresolved disagreement into forward motion without
  requiring anyone to be convinced: "By the time you're at this point, no one can know
  the answer for sure, and you'll probably get a quick yes." Bezos applies it to
  himself, downward: he greenlit an Amazon Studios original he disbelieved in rather
  than force the team to convince him.
- **Resist proxies.** "The process is not the thing. ... do we own the process or does
  the process own us?" Process compliance, surveys, and metrics are stand-ins for the
  outcome; in Day 2 companies the stand-in becomes the goal.
- **Escalate misalignment instead of grinding.** "'You've worn me down' is an awful
  decision-making process. It's slow and de-energizing. Go for quick escalation
  instead."
- **Day 2 is what slow decision quality buys you**: "Day 2 is stasis. Followed by
  irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death."

### Verbatim quotes

> "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible — one-way
> doors — and these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great
> deliberation and consultation. ... But most decisions aren't like that — they are
> changeable, reversible — they're two-way doors. ... Type 2 decisions can and should be
> made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups." — 2015 letter

> "Any companies that habitually use the light-weight Type 2 decision-making process to
> make Type 1 decisions go extinct before they get large." — 2015 letter, footnote

> "Most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information
> you wish you had. If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow."
> — 2016 letter

> "If you're good at course correcting, being wrong may be less costly than you think,
> whereas being slow is going to be expensive for sure." — 2016 letter

> "Day 2 companies make high-quality decisions, but they make high-quality decisions
> slowly." — 2016 letter

> "Good process serves you so you can serve customers. But if you're not watchful, the
> process can become the thing. ... It's not that rare to hear a junior leader defend a
> bad outcome with something like, 'Well, we followed the process.'" — 2016 letter

### Agent translation

- Before demanding more evidence, classify the door. Most agent-level choices (naming,
  structure, tool selection, draft content, refactors with tests) are two-way doors:
  decide now at 70% and correct later. Reserve deliberation for genuinely one-way moves
  (deletes without backup, external sends, prod mutations, public commitments).
- "We followed the process" is the exact shape of receipt-maximizing behavior. A
  perfect audit trail on a bad outcome is Day 2 thinking.
- Both light and heavy processes have a failure mode (footnote quote above): the skill
  is classification, not a universal bias toward either speed or caution.

## 4. Annie Duke — Thinking in Bets

**Source:** "The Resulting Fallacy Is Ruining Your Decisions" (Stuart Firestein
interviews Annie Duke), Nautilus, Nov 2017 —
https://nautil.us/the-resulting-fallacy-is-ruining-your-decisions-236901
**Source:** "How To Make The Right Decisions Even When You Don't Have All The Facts" —
https://www.annieduke.com/how-to-make-the-right-decisions-even-when-you-dont-have-all-the-facts/
(both drawing on *Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the
Facts*, Portfolio, 2018)

### Distilled principles

- **"Resulting"** is judging decision quality by outcome quality. In any domain with
  luck and hidden information (poker, startups, product bets), a good decision can lose
  and a bad decision can win. Grading yourself on single outcomes teaches the wrong
  lessons in both directions.
- Chess-thinking vs poker-thinking: "In chess, if I lose a game, it's pretty certain
  that I made a bad decision somewhere. ... But it is a very unreasonable strategy in
  poker." Startup/product decisions are poker, not chess — outcome feedback is noisy,
  so demanding certainty before acting is incoherent.
- **Hidden information is permanent.** "We can't see everything. We haven't experienced
  everything. ... the job of the decider is to reduce the uncertainty as much as they
  possibly can, but to understand that they're always working within a range." 100%
  information is not on the menu; the question is only how much reduction is worth its
  cost in time.
- Pete Carroll's Super Bowl interception: below-2% failure odds, sound clock logic —
  and headlines calling it "worst play in Super Bowl history." "The outcome was
  irrelevant to the decision quality." Expect good fast decisions to sometimes look
  terrible in hindsight; do not let that push you into over-proofing the next one.
- **Sample size matters for outcome-reading**: 15 car crashes in a year says something
  about decision quality; one crash says almost nothing. React to patterns of failure,
  not single failures.
- Practical debiasing: evaluate decisions with the outcome hidden ("I may not tell you
  the decision I made, because that might infect you"). Review the state of knowledge at
  decision time, not the result.

### Verbatim quotes

> "You can think about it as creating too tight a relationship between the quality of
> the outcome and the quality of the decision. You can't use outcome quality as a
> perfect signal of decision quality, not with a small sample size anyway." — Nautilus

> "If I lose a hand, I may have played the hand literally perfectly and still lost
> because there's this luck element to it. The problem is that we're all resulters at
> heart." — Nautilus

> "Don't be so hard on yourself when things go badly and don't be so proud of yourself
> when they go well." — Nautilus

> "The job of the decider is to reduce the uncertainty as much as they possibly can, but
> to understand that they're always working within a range and they have limited control
> over how things turn out on any given try." — Nautilus

### Agent translation

- A single bad outcome after a fast decision does not prove the fast process was wrong,
  and it does not justify ratcheting every future decision up to maximum proof. That
  ratchet is resulting.
- The correct response to permanent uncertainty is probabilistic action plus review of
  *decision process* — not stacking receipts until uncertainty feels like zero. The
  feeling of zero is unavailable; chasing it only spends time.

## 5. Marty Cagan — Discovery vs Delivery (the speed/rigor split)

**Source:** "Discovery vs. Delivery," Silicon Valley Product Group, Oct 2015 —
https://www.svpg.com/discovery-vs-delivery/
**Source:** "Product Discovery," Silicon Valley Product Group —
https://www.svpg.com/product-discovery/

### Distilled principles

- Two simultaneous obligations pull in opposite directions: **"learn fast"** (discovery)
  and **"release with confidence"** (delivery). Strong teams do not average the two into
  one medium-rigor process; they run different standards for different work.
- **Discovery work is allowed to be throwaway-fast.** Prototypes, experiments, and
  opt-in tests exist to generate evidence cheaply; "the majority of the techniques don't
  even require the developer's time." Applying production standards to discovery work is
  waste.
- **Delivery work earns its rigor** — after evidence exists: "the purpose of product
  discovery is to make sure we have some evidence that when we ask the engineers to
  build production-quality software, it won't be a wasted effort."
- Building production-grade software for an unvalidated idea "is a recipe for big
  waste" — most engineering time goes into productization (scalability, regression
  tests, instrumentation, i18n, maintainability), so spending it before evidence is the
  costliest mistake available.
- For very early startups the split collapses toward speed: "If you are an early stage
  startup and you have no customers ... it may be premature to even be creating
  production-quality software."
- Reserve the word "product" for things you can run a business on; don't let the
  vocabulary of production quality leak backward into experiments and inflate their
  required rigor.

### Verbatim quotes

> "So we need to simultaneously learn fast and also release with confidence."
> — "Discovery vs. Delivery"

> "Doing all this work when the product manager isn't even sure this is the solution the
> customer wants or needs is a recipe for big waste." — "Discovery vs. Delivery"

> "If you are an early stage startup and you have no customers, then of course this is
> not really an issue (and it may be premature to even be creating production-quality
> software)." — "Discovery vs. Delivery"

> "If we want to move fast and discover quickly, we use discovery techniques and opt-in
> customers. Once we have collected some evidence that we know the solution we need to
> build, we allow our engineers to build the 'production-quality' software as they see
> fit." — "Discovery vs. Delivery"

### Agent translation

- Ask of every task: is this discovery or delivery? Exploratory scripts, drafts,
  analyses, and spikes get discovery standards (fast, disposable, minimal proof).
  Only the validated path gets delivery standards.
- The two-standard model beats one universal standard. An agent that applies its
  delivery-grade verification ritual to every throwaway question is burning the
  majority cost category (productization effort) on work that needed none.

## 6. Richard P. Gabriel — "Worse is Better"

**Source:** "The Rise of Worse is Better" (from "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win
Big," 1989/1991) — https://www.dreamsongs.com/RiseOfWorseIsBetter.html

### Distilled principles

- Two design philosophies: **"the right thing"** (MIT approach: correctness,
  consistency, and completeness are non-negotiable) vs **"worse is better"** (New Jersey
  approach: "Simplicity is the most important consideration in a design," and
  correctness/completeness may be traded away to keep the implementation simple).
- Gabriel's uncomfortable conclusion: worse-is-better "has better survival
  characteristics than the-right-thing." Unix and C — 50-80% solutions that shipped,
  ported, and spread — beat technically superior systems that aimed for 100%.
- The viral dynamics of shipping early: "the worse-is-better software first will gain
  acceptance, second will condition its users to expect less, and third will be improved
  to a point that is almost the right thing." Getting real usage first buys the
  improvement budget later.
- Precondition: "It is important to remember that the initial virus has to be basically
  good." Worse-is-better is not a license for broken software; it is a priority ordering
  where implementation simplicity outranks completeness.
- The PC loser-ing story: Unix pushed interrupt-handling complexity onto the caller
  (check an error code and retry) instead of solving it "right" in the kernel. Simple
  implementation, slightly worse interface — and it won.

### Verbatim quotes

> "Simplicity is the most important consideration in a design."
> — worse-is-better tenets

> "It is slightly better to be simple than correct." — worse-is-better tenets
> (deliberately caricatured, and still, Gabriel argues, the winning strategy)

> "I believe that worse-is-better, even in its strawman form, has better survival
> characteristics than the-right-thing."

> "Both early Unix and C compilers had simple structures, are easy to port, require few
> machine resources to run, and provide about 50%-80% of what you want from an operating
> system and programming language."

> "Unix and C are the ultimate computer viruses."

### Agent translation

- A 50-80% solution in users' hands today outcompetes a 100% solution later. When an
  agent holds back work to reach "the right thing," it is betting against the
  historically winning strategy.
- Keep the tradeoff ordering explicit: simplicity of what ships > completeness of what
  ships. Cut scope, not shipping.

## 7. Donald Knuth — Premature Optimization (the full quote)

**Source:** Donald E. Knuth, "Structured Programming with go to Statements," ACM
Computing Surveys 6:4 (1974). Quoted and contextualized in Malte Skarupke, "Revisiting
Knuth's 'Premature Optimization' Paper" —
https://probablydance.com/2025/06/19/revisiting-knuths-premature-optimization-paper/

### Distilled principles

- The famous sentence has two halves. The quoted half: "premature optimization is the
  root of all evil." The forgotten half: "Yet we should not pass up our opportunities in
  that critical 3%." The doctrine is *targeted* effort, not zero effort.
- The mechanism of the evil: effort on "the speed of noncritical parts" has "a strong
  negative impact when debugging and maintenance are considered." Wasted rigor isn't
  neutral — it actively damages the system's changeability.
- **Intuition about what is critical is unreliable**: "It is often a mistake to make a
  priori judgments about what parts of a program are really critical, since the
  universal experience of programmers who have been using measurement tools has been
  that their intuitive guesses fail." Identify the critical 3% by measurement, then
  invest there.
- Knuth also cuts the other way: a 12% win "easily obtained, is never considered
  marginal" in real engineering — but "Of course I wouldn't bother making such
  optimizations on a one-shot job." Effort should scale with how long the artifact will
  live.

### Verbatim quotes

> "Programmers waste enormous amounts of time thinking about, or worrying about, the
> speed of noncritical parts of their programs, and these attempts at efficiency
> actually have a strong negative impact when debugging and maintenance are considered.
> We should forget about small efficiencies, say about 97% of the time: premature
> optimization is the root of all evil." — Knuth, 1974

> "Yet we should not pass up our opportunities in that critical 3%." — Knuth, 1974

> "It is often a mistake to make a priori judgments about what parts of a program are
> really critical ... intuitive guesses fail." — Knuth, 1974

> "Of course I wouldn't bother making such optimizations on a one-shot job." — Knuth, 1974

### Agent translation

- Generalize beyond speed: premature *anything* (abstraction, hardening, proof,
  generality) on the noncritical 97% is the same evil. Find the critical 3% empirically
  — from real failures and real user pain — then be rigorous there.
- "One-shot job" is a load-bearing category. Most agent outputs are one-shot or
  short-lived; they do not deserve durable-artifact investment.

## 8. Martin Fowler / Extreme Programming — YAGNI

**Source:** Martin Fowler, "Yagni," 26 May 2015 — https://martinfowler.com/bliki/Yagni.html

### Distilled principles

- YAGNI ("You Aren't Gonna Need It"): do not build a presumptive capability now because
  you believe you'll need it later. Born on the C3 project: Kent Beck answering every
  "we'll soon need X" with "you aren't going to need it."
- **You're probably wrong about needing it.** Fowler cites Kohavi et al.: even with
  careful up-front analysis, only ~1/3 of features improved the metrics they were meant
  to improve — so the base-rate odds that presumptive work is wasted are "at least 2/3."
- Four costs of building early: **cost of build** (wasted effort if wrong), **cost of
  delay** (the valuable thing you didn't ship meanwhile), **cost of carry** (the extra
  complexity everyone pays to work around), and **cost of repair** (wrong-shape code
  when the need finally arrives).
- The mentoring move: "imagine the refactoring you would have to do later to introduce
  the capability when it's needed. Often that thought experiment is enough to convince
  them that it won't be significantly more expensive to add it later." Later-cost is
  usually overestimated; now-cost (delay + carry) is usually underestimated.
- YAGNI is only cheap if the codebase stays malleable — it "only works well with
  practices that keep the cost of change low" (simple design, refactoring, tests). Speed
  and sloppiness are not the same thing.

### Verbatim quotes

> "Yagni ... is a statement that some capability we presume our software needs in the
> future should not be built now because 'you aren't gonna need it'."

> "The common reason why people build presumptive features is because they think it will
> be cheaper to build it now rather than build it later. But that cost comparison has to
> be made at least against the cost of delay, preferably factoring in the probability
> that you're building an unnecessary feature, for which your odds are at least 2/3."

### Agent translation

- Every speculative "while I'm here, I'll also handle..." expansion is a presumptive
  feature with 2/3 odds of being waste, plus guaranteed delay and carry costs on the
  thing that was actually asked for.
- The refactoring thought-experiment is a great agent check: "if this need shows up
  later, how expensive is it really to add then?" Usually: cheap. So don't build now.

## 9. Dan McKinley — "Choose Boring Technology"

**Source:** Dan McKinley, "Choose Boring Technology," 2015 —
https://mcfunley.com/choose-boring-technology

### Distilled principles

- **Innovation tokens:** "every company gets about three innovation tokens. You can
  spend these however you want, but the supply is fixed for a long while." Every novel
  technology choice spends one. Spend them only on things core to the mission.
- Boring ≠ bad. "MySQL is boring. Postgres is boring. PHP is boring. ... The nice thing
  about boringness (so constrained) is that the capabilities of these things are well
  understood. But more importantly, their failure modes are well understood."
- New/shiny choices trade known unknowns for **unknown unknowns** — failure modes nobody
  on the team can predict or budget for.
- The company mission is the thing that deserves novelty: "devoting any of your limited
  attention to innovating ssh is an excellent way to fail."

### Verbatim quotes

> "Let's say every company gets about three innovation tokens. You can spend these
> however you want, but the supply is fixed for a long while. ... the general tendency
> is to overestimate the contents of your wallet."

> "'Boring' should not be conflated with 'bad.' ... the capabilities of these things are
> well understood. But more importantly, their failure modes are well understood."

### Agent translation

- Default to the boring, well-understood approach (standard library, existing pattern
  in the repo, known tool) unless the novel choice is the actual point of the task. A
  3-person startup — or an agent inside one — has almost no innovation tokens to spend
  on infrastructure, process, or bespoke machinery.
- Elaborate custom verification harnesses, novel abstractions, and clever meta-systems
  are innovation-token spends on non-mission work.

## 10. Paul Graham — "Do Things That Don't Scale"

**Source:** Paul Graham, "Do Things that Don't Scale," July 2013 —
https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

### Distilled principles

- "Startups take off because the founders make them take off" — with manual, laborious,
  unscalable pushes (recruiting users one at a time, Airbnb founders photographing
  hosts' apartments door to door). Waiting for a scalable, automated, defensible motion
  before acting is how launches die.
- **Don't judge larval-stage work by established-stage standards.** "They unconsciously
  judge larval startups by the standards of established ones. They're like someone
  looking at a newborn baby and concluding 'there's no way this tiny creature could ever
  accomplish anything.'"
- "Insanely great" means something different early: "It's not the product that should be
  insanely great, but the experience of being your user. ... you can and should give
  users an insanely great experience with an early, incomplete, buggy product, if you
  make up the difference with attentiveness."
- Manual-first is also a learning strategy: over-engaging with early users "is a
  necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product good."

### Verbatim quotes

> "Startups take off because the founders make them take off."

> "You can and should give users an insanely great experience with an early, incomplete,
> buggy product, if you make up the difference with attentiveness."

> "Over-engaging with early users is not just a permissible technique for getting growth
> rolling. For most successful startups it's a necessary part of the feedback loop that
> makes the product good."

### Agent translation

- The engineering analog: hand-run the process, hardcode the config, do the migration
  manually, answer the question with a one-off script. Automation, generality, and
  scale-proofing come after the manual version proves the loop is worth automating.
- "Early, incomplete, buggy + attentive" beats "complete, polished, late." The agent's
  attentiveness (fast follow-ups, quick fixes) is the substitute for up-front
  completeness.

## 11. Boyd's OODA Loop, John Carmack, and id Software — Speed of Iteration

**Source:** Jeff Atwood, "Boyd's Law of Iteration," Coding Horror, Feb 2007 (quoting
Roger Sessions, "A Better Path to Enterprise Architecture") —
https://blog.codinghorror.com/boyds-law-of-iteration/
**Source:** John Carmack interviews archive (Fabien Sanglard's collection) —
https://fabiensanglard.net/fd_proxy/doom3/pdfs/johnc-interviews.pdf
**Source:** "id Software Principles" (Romero's 2016 talk, annotated) —
https://charlesboury.fr/articles/id-software-principles.html

### Distilled principles

- **Boyd's Law of Iteration: "speed of iteration beats quality of iteration."** Boyd
  asked why the technically inferior F-86 beat the superior MiG-15 nine times out of
  ten: the F-86's hydraulic stick let its pilot cycle the observe-orient-decide-act loop
  faster. "The primary determinant to winning dogfights was not observing, orienting,
  planning, or acting better ... [it] was observing, orienting, planning, and acting
  faster."
- The corollary that matters for tooling: a small reduction in per-iteration cost
  (hydraulic vs manual stick) compounds into decisive advantage. Invest in whatever
  lowers the cost of one loop; do not invest in perfecting any single loop's output.
- Atwood's software mapping: small fast unit tests, usability tests that "make small
  changes every two weeks and quickly discard what isn't working," short agile
  iterations, failing early and often, concise evolving specs. "When in doubt, iterate
  faster."
- Carmack on decision volume over decision brilliance: quality emerges from many quick
  sound calls, not one perfect one (quote below).
- Romero on why id shipped bug-free hits with tiny teams: assembly-era habits forced
  "code a little bit, run it, code a little bit, run it. Anything that's wrong, you'll
  know immediately" — tight loops as the *source* of quality, not a threat to it.

### Verbatim quotes

> "Speed of iteration, Boyd suggested, beats quality of iteration."
> — Roger Sessions, quoted in Coding Horror

> "The MiG-15 pilot didn't lose because he got outfought. He lost because he got
> out-OODAed." — Roger Sessions, quoted in Coding Horror (loop rendered as "OOPA" in
> the original article text)

> "When in doubt, iterate faster." — Jeff Atwood, Coding Horror

> "It's not the one brilliant decision, it's the 500 smart decisions that really make
> things good. It's more a matter of being able to keep making smart decisions." — John
> Carmack, interviews archive

> "So our testing loops, our iteration times were really short. ... Anything that's
> wrong, you'll know immediately and it's not like an hour worth of work that you now
> have to spend another hour trying to figure out what happened." — John Romero,
> Wolfenstein 3D postmortem

### Agent translation

- The unit of optimization is the loop, not the artifact. An agent that doubles the
  quality of one pass while halving the number of passes has made itself strictly worse
  under Boyd's law.
- Heavy per-step ceremony (long proofs, exhaustive receipts, full re-verification) is
  the manual flight stick: each cycle costs a little more, and the compounding cost
  loses the dogfight.

## 12. Startup Genome — Premature Scaling, the #1 Startup Killer

**Source:** JF Gauthier / Startup Genome, "Premature Scaling: A Deep Dive," Sept 2011
(elaborating the Startup Genome Report Extra on Premature Scaling) —
https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive
**Source (commentary):** Steve Blank, "It's Not How Big It Is — It's How Well It
Performs: The Startup Genome Compass" —
https://steveblank.com/2011/08/29/it%E2%80%99s-not-how-big-it-is-%E2%80%93-it%E2%80%99s-how-well-it-performs-the-startup-genome-compass/

### Distilled principles

- From a dataset of 3,200+ high-growth startups: "the primary cause of failure is
  premature scaling, an affliction that 70% of startups in our dataset possess."
- The outcomes are brutal: "No startup that scaled prematurely passed the 100,000 user
  mark." 93% never broke $100k revenue/month. "Startups that scale properly grow about
  20 times faster than startups that scale prematurely."
- Definition: premature scaling = any dimension (customer, product, team, financials,
  business model) operating at a *later* stage than the startup's actual, evidence-based
  stage. E.g., a 30-40 person team before any customers; heavy acquisition spend before
  product/market fit.
- **Over-engineering is a measured symptom**: "Inconsistent startups write 3.4 times
  more lines of code in the discovery phase and 2.25 times more code in efficiency
  stage." Product-dimension premature scaling includes "Building a product without
  having validated problem/solution fit, Investing into scalability of the product
  before product/market fit, Adding lots of 'nice to have' features."
- The focus inversion: in discovery, 80% of consistent (healthy) startups focus on
  discovering a problem space, while 60% of inconsistent startups are already focused on
  validating a product — rigor pointed at the wrong stage.

### Verbatim quotes

> "Our latest research found that the primary cause of failure is premature scaling, an
> affliction that 70% of startups in our dataset possess."

> "No startup that scaled prematurely passed the 100,000 user mark."

> "Inconsistent startups write 3.4 times more lines of code in the discovery phase and
> 2.25 times more code in efficiency stage."

> "It's widely believed amongst startup thought leaders, that successful startups
> succeed because they are good searchers and failed startups achieve failure by
> efficiently executing the irrelevant."

### Agent translation

- "3.4x more code in discovery" is the empirical fingerprint of over-engineering as a
  killer: more output, more machinery, more apparent rigor — 20x slower actual growth.
  Volume of work is inversely correlated with survival when the stage is wrong.
- "Failure by efficiently executing the irrelevant" is the precise description of an
  agent maximizing proof and polish on an unvalidated direction.

## 13. 37signals / Basecamp — "Getting Real"

**Source:** 37signals, *Getting Real* (2006), free online edition —
https://basecamp.com/gettingreal — chapters: "Build Less"
(https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/02.1-build-less), "Fix Time and Budget, Flex Scope"
(https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/02.4-fix-time-and-budget-flex-scope), "Embrace
Constraints" (https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/03.4-embrace-constraints), "Scale Later"
(https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/04.5-scale-later), "Half, Not Half-Assed"
(https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/05.1-half-not-half-assed)

### Distilled principles

- **Build less / underdo the competition:** "Do less than your competitors to beat them.
  Solve the simple problems and leave the hairy, difficult, nasty problems to everyone
  else." Less features, less options, less meetings, less abstractions, less promises.
- **Half, not half-assed:** "Take whatever you think your product should be and cut it
  in half. Pare features down until you're left with only the most essential ones. Then
  do it again." Basecamp launched as just the messages section — real usage then decided
  what came next.
- **Fix time and budget, flex scope:** "Never throw more time or money at a problem,
  just scale back the scope. ... There's always time to add stuff later — later is
  eternal, now is fleeting."
- **Embrace constraints:** "Constraints drive innovation and force focus." Basecamp was
  built by a tiny distributed team with no funding, around client work — and the book
  credits the constraints for the product's shape. "Constraints are often advantages in
  disguise."
- **Scale later:** "Will my app scale when millions of people start using it? Ya know
  what? Wait until that actually happens." Basecamp ran on one server for its first
  year, a setup that took a week. "The bigger problem isn't scaling, it's getting to the
  point where you have to scale."
- Feature blight (quoted approvingly in "Embrace Constraints"): "creeping elegance"
  gradually "blurs the true outline of the product while it drains its sap," and the
  antidote is the constricting deadline.

### Verbatim quotes

> "What you really want to do is build half a product that kicks ass." — "Half, Not
> Half-Assed"

> "Take whatever you think your product should be and cut it in half. Pare features down
> until you're left with only the most essential ones. Then do it again." — "Half, Not
> Half-Assed"

> "Never throw more time or money at a problem, just scale back the scope." — "Fix Time
> and Budget, Flex Scope"

> "There's always time to add stuff later — later is eternal, now is fleeting." — "Fix
> Time and Budget, Flex Scope"

> "In retrospect, we're quite glad we didn't delay launch for months in order to create
> 'the perfect setup.'" — "Scale Later"

> "Constraints are often advantages in disguise. Forget about venture capital, long
> release cycles, and quick hires. Instead, work with what you have." — "Embrace
> Constraints"

### Agent translation

- When effort exceeds budget, the mandated move is to cut scope — never to silently
  extend time. "Half, not half-assed" gives the cut's direction: fewer things done
  properly, not everything done shabbily.
- "The perfect setup" (staging, clusters, harnesses, frameworks) before real load is
  the engineering shape of premature scaling. One server, one week, first year.

## 14. David Packard / Bryce Roberts — Death by Indigestion, Not Starvation

**Source:** Bryce Roberts, "Death by Indigestion," Sept 2017 —
https://bryce.medium.com/death-by-indigestion-ae0d64e7648f
(The aphorism is usually attributed to HP co-founder David Packard — "More companies die
of indigestion than starvation," *The HP Way* — though Roberts' post credits Bill
Hewlett. Both attributions circulate; the HP origin is not in dispute.)

### Distilled principles

- **"More companies die of indigestion than starvation."** The default diagnosis for
  what ails a startup is "more" — more money, more people, more product, more process.
  The HP warning is that "more" is what kills.
- Roberts, citing Scott Sonenshein's research (*Stretch*): "With abundance, people treat
  resources as what they appear on the surface, utilizing them in traditional ways. ...
  With constraints, things unfold MUCH differently." Scarcity forces unconventional,
  higher-leverage use of what you have.
- Fred Wilson, quoted in the post: "I believe that excess capital makes companies weak
  and unfocused." Abundance is a discipline problem, not a blessing.

### Verbatim quotes

> "More companies die of indigestion than starvation." — HP founders' aphorism, as
> quoted by Bryce Roberts

> "In the minds of many, the answer to what ails them defaults to more. We see this in
> startups all the time. More money to hire more people to build more product to drive
> more growth. A simple formula, a soothing siren song but ... dead wrong." — Bryce
> Roberts

> "I believe that excess capital makes companies weak and unfocused." — Fred Wilson,
> quoted by Roberts

### Agent translation

- Agents die of indigestion too: more checks, more artifacts, more parallel workstreams,
  more machinery. The bottleneck at seed stage is never "not enough process"; adding
  process is eating more, not digesting better.

## 15. Synthesis — "How Much Rigor Does This Decision Actually Deserve?"

A single framework distilled from the sources above. Four questions, asked in order,
decide the rigor budget for any decision or piece of work. Then a calibration table and
the standing failure modes.

### The four questions

**Q1. Is this a one-way or a two-way door? (Reversibility)** — Bezos 2015/2016 letters.
- Two-way door (revertible commit, renameable name, redoable draft, re-runnable
  analysis): "can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small
  groups." Minimal rigor. Decide and move.
- One-way door (irreversible delete, external send, prod mutation, public commitment,
  money spent): "methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation." This is
  the only class that deserves heavy proof.
- Guard both ways: Bezos' footnote warns that habitually using the light process on
  true one-way doors is fatal too. The skill is classification, not a blanket bias.
- Supporting: Fowler's refactoring thought-experiment ("how expensive is it *really*
  to add/fix later?") is the practical reversibility probe.

**Q2. What does being wrong actually cost? (Cost of error vs cost of delay)** — Bezos,
Fowler, Knuth, Cagan.
- Bezos: "If you're good at course correcting, being wrong may be less costly than you
  think, whereas being slow is going to be expensive for sure." Price the error against
  the delay, not against zero.
- Fowler: the presumptive-work comparison must include cost of delay and cost of carry,
  "factoring in the probability that you're building an unnecessary feature, for which
  your odds are at least 2/3."
- Knuth: rigor invested in the noncritical 97% isn't just wasted — it has "a strong
  negative impact" on everything that comes after. And intuition about what's critical
  "fails"; find the critical 3% by measurement, not by anxiety.
- Cagan: most engineering cost is productization; spending it pre-evidence "is a recipe
  for big waste."

**Q3. How much information is actually available, and at what price? (Information)** —
Bezos, Duke, Blank.
- Bezos: act at "somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had"; waiting for
  90% means "you're probably being slow."
- Duke: some information is *permanently* hidden — "the job of the decider is to reduce
  the uncertainty as much as they possibly can, but to understand that they're always
  working within a range." Past ~70%, further proof buys feeling, not knowledge.
- Blank: "There are no facts inside your building." Information that can only come from
  contact with users/reality cannot be substituted by more internal analysis, more
  self-review, or more receipts. If the missing 30% lives outside, ship something and
  ask.

**Q4. Which option maximizes learning per unit time? (Learning speed)** — Ries, Boyd,
Blank, Graham, Romero.
- Ries: the unit of progress is validated learning; gear everything "to accelerate that
  feedback loop."
- Boyd's law: "speed of iteration beats quality of iteration." When two options are
  otherwise close, take the one that gets real feedback sooner.
- Graham: manual, unscalable, incomplete-but-attentive beats automated, complete, late —
  because early contact "is a necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product
  good."
- Carmack: outcomes come from "the 500 smart decisions," so protect the ability to make
  many decisions rather than perfecting any single one.

### Calibration table

| Situation | Door | Cost of wrong | Rigor deserved | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Naming, structure, drafts, internal docs | Two-way | Trivial | None beyond taste; decide instantly | Bezos T2; YAGNI |
| Code with tests, behind review, revertible | Two-way | Low (fast correction) | Light: make it work, verify the changed seam, ship | Bezos 70%; Boyd; Ries |
| Choice of tool/tech/abstraction | Two-way but carries cost | Medium (carry cost) | Default boring; novelty only on mission-critical need | McKinley; Fowler |
| Speculative capability "we'll need later" | N/A — don't walk through | 2/3 odds pure waste + delay + carry | Zero. Don't build it | Fowler; Startup Genome |
| Scaling/hardening before real load | N/A — premature | Measured killer (70% of failed startups) | Zero until evidence of load | Startup Genome; Getting Real |
| Perf/robustness in the critical 3% (measured) | Varies | High, proven | Real rigor — after measurement identifies it | Knuth |
| Irreversible delete, external send, prod data, money | One-way | High/permanent | Full deliberation, consultation, proof | Bezos T1 |
| Direction/spec choice with zero user contact yet | One-way-ish (compounds) | Iridium | Don't add rigor — add reality contact; smallest testable version out | Blank; Ries; Cagan |

### Standing failure modes this framework exists to catch

1. **Proof-maximizing on two-way doors.** Producing receipts, exhaustive verification,
   and defensive documentation for decisions that cost less to redo than to prove.
   (Bezos: Day-2 slowness; Knuth: negative impact of misplaced effort.)
2. **"We followed the process."** Treating process compliance as the outcome. (Bezos:
   "The process is not the thing.")
3. **Resulting-driven ratchets.** One bad outcome after a fast decision → permanently
   heavier process. Judge the decision process against information available at the
   time; react to failure *patterns*, not single failures. (Duke.)
4. **Efficiently executing the irrelevant.** Flawless implementation of an unvalidated
   direction — the Iridium play, the 3.4x-more-code discovery-phase startup. (Blank;
   Startup Genome.)
5. **Indigestion.** Adding machinery — more checks, more harnesses, more parallel
   process — as the default answer. More is what kills. (Packard/Roberts; McKinley's
   innovation tokens.)
6. **Perfection before contact.** Holding work back until it is "the right thing."
   The 50-80% version that ships wins. (Gabriel; Graham; Getting Real.)
7. **Scope-hold instead of scope-cut.** When over budget, extending time to keep full
   scope. The correct move is always "half, not half-assed." (37signals.)

### The one-paragraph version (for the skill)

Classify the door first. If it's two-way — and at seed stage almost everything is —
decide at 50-70% of the information you wish you had, use the boring option, build half,
ship the rudimentary version, and spend the saved time on another loop: speed of
iteration beats quality of iteration, and being wrong is cheaper than being slow when
you course-correct fast. Reserve real rigor for the two places it pays: genuinely
irreversible moves, and the measured critical 3%. Never grade the decision by one
outcome, never substitute internal proof for outside contact, and when the budget is
tight, cut scope — never quality, never the deadline.
