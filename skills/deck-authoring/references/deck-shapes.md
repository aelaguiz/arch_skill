# Deck shapes by audience and purpose

The rules in `SKILL.md` hold for every deck. What changes with the reader and
the deck's job is density, length, what opens the deck, and whether slides
carry verdicts. Pick the shape from who will read it and what they will do
with it, and blend shapes when a deck serves two jobs.

## An advisor or investor meeting

- **Density:** the lowest of any deck. "Focus on clarity around the few most
  fundamental things rather than lots of detail."
- **Opens with** the reader's worry, answered, then the story. If they are
  coming in worried about money, money comes first.
- **Numbers:** measured results only. A seed-stage company does not put
  forecasts, profit-and-loss projections, or simulations at the center. A
  model is most credible shown plainly, like the spreadsheet, with its
  assumptions marked.
- **Length:** a short main story, roughly ten to twenty slides, with
  everything else in a linked appendix deck.
- **Tone:** a friendly meeting. No caveat footers; one sources slide at the
  back; speaker notes clean of anything the reader shouldn't see.
- **Words:** Amir's framing, not the advisor's. An advisor's ideas are one
  input, never the goal. No internal codenames, no defensive phrases.
- **Before it goes out:** strip internal prep slides and names from shared
  footers. Draft any email; never send a deck outside the company without his
  explicit go.

## The team's weekly focus deck

- **Co-authored.** Build the front simply and leave later slides as titled
  placeholders until he settles the front. "You went too far without me." His
  thinking out loud is thinking, not an instruction; he did not name owners
  just because he mentioned names.
- **His words.** The whys, the outcomes, and the priorities are his lines.
- **Journeys:** today's journey and the intended one, each first at a glance
  with no callouts, then in detail. The team should recognize their own app.
- **Ambition:** the goals cover the whole scope he set, not a smaller version.
  No slides spent on work already shipped.
- **Hygiene:** no candid remarks about teammates; review copies go to Amir
  alone; he trims before it goes to the team.

## A decision or approval deck

- **Opens with** his goals as you read them ("correct it if we read you
  wrong"), then the priorities ranked by the size of the opportunity.
- **One decision per slide**, each carrying its status in the kicker, the
  recommendation as the headline, the economics (cost beside the payers it
  brings; for moving money, where from, where to, how much), how it differs
  from what is already planned, and today beside the change.
- **Upside first.** Find what is working and how to get more of it; bugs go in
  one calm, linked section unless one of them stops growth.
- **Commit.** Give the likely read and a recommendation on every open
  question. About 70% sure is enough to recommend at this stage.
- **Long is fine** when every slide is one clear decision.
- **Planning is not deciding.** While he is still shaping a plan, explain it
  and ask for nothing.

## A research deck

- **Complete.** One case per slide, the real screens in journey order, the
  real numbers. "a complete unpack, not one slide with fucking assertions".
- **Findings, few verdicts.** "give me all the information you can find. I
  will make my own judgements." A short version still opens the deck.
- **Their business, not ours.** A report about competitors covers the
  competitors; our opportunity goes in a separate deck or section when asked.
- **Estimates over "unknown"**, each labeled as an estimate with its company,
  population, and period. No coverage maps, question numbers, or method pages.
- **Long is fine** (sixty to a hundred slides) when the front is simple and
  every part has a divider.
  Send it in parts as each part reads cold rather than holding the whole deck.

## A design or proposal deck

- **Four parts, in order:** the goal and what it must account for; the user
  journey; the mocks in journey order; the supporting analysis. Superseded
  drafts go to the back.
- **Today, then ours.** Show what exists today (ours or a competitor's, real
  captures), then each proposal as a mock in our app's own style, as many
  slides per idea as the idea needs. Several mocked takes side by side work
  when he is choosing.
- **Future-facing.** No built or not-built labels, no readiness slides, no
  prototype bugs.
- **Mocks start from what is built.** A live capture or the PR's screenshots
  before any design file; label every concept.

## A deck that orients Amir on something new

- **Short and visual.** "Make it so that it's not text-dense and it's more
  visual and easy to follow for me."
- **Describe before prescribing.** While he is still forming the picture,
  show what exists and how it works; hold next steps until he asks for them.
- **Assume no memory of it.** "assume I completely forgot what the fuck is in
  this entire feature stack over the last week."

## A deck for a teammate

- **For a designer catching up:** tell the chain of investigation that led to
  the plan, and put the visuals up front.
- **For a non-technical teammate:** make it simple, with worked examples, the
  literal prompts to use, which model to choose, and an example of the result.
- **For someone reviewing a proposal:** a deck with the rationale, not a PR.

## How dense, measured

Word count alone does not separate good decks from bad ones. Measured on the
decks he praised, the median words on a slide's face run about:

| Deck | Words per slide | Carried by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advisor or strategy story | 40 to 60 | a picture on most slides |
| Team and operating decks | 60 to 130 | a chart or real screen on most slides |
| Research and daily decision decks | 120 to 170 | tables and labeled screenshot strips |

What the good ones share: a one-line takeaway headline, a picture of the thing
on almost every slide, every line readable alone, and density held in tables
and labeled screenshots rather than prose. A deck rebuilt with the same words
but a visual form chosen for each slide went from rejected to his model deck.

## How his apparent contradictions resolve

- **"Overly concise" and "too dense."** Both were fixed the same way: more
  slides, each holding one step or one change in full sentences. Add slides,
  not words per slide, and never remove the context a reader needs.
- **"200 clear slides" and "about 20 slides."** Both say don't stuff. A
  decision deck is long because each idea gets its own slide; a meeting deck
  has a short main story and an appendix deck.
- **"Make it visual" and "too stylized."** Visual means the real thing shown
  faithfully. A model whose credibility matters is a plain table; a strategic
  idea can be an illustration.
- **"Just a table" and "unreadable rows."** Tables hold exact values and
  atomic data. Findings and recommendations never get compressed into rows.
- **Verdicts.** Decision decks recommend. Research and orientation decks
  present what is known until he opens a decision. No deck project-manages.
- **Caveats.** Hedges never go on a conversation slide. Edge cases someone
  will build from belong in a spec section. One line of framing that keeps a
  number honest ("worst case", "hypothetical") stays on the slide.
- **The past.** History is evidence of what is achievable at our best. It is
  never the frame the deck is narrated against.
- **An early "great".** A first "This is great" approves the concept and the
  shape, not the density. He rejected the same mock's six-row recommendation
  table a day later.
- **One deck or a new one.** Updates to a project go into that project's deck.
  A new research question, or a plan that would be buried, gets its own deck,
  linked from both.
