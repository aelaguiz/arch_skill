# Deck Authoring

Build decks Amir can flip through, not decks he has to study, and get them to
him fast. He is usually waiting on the deck to do his own work, so the wait is
part of what the deck costs him. He opens it in the middle of other work, often
about work he did not watch, and jumps to whatever slide catches his eye. Every
slide has to make sense to him in a few seconds, on its own, with nothing else
open. Your job is to do the translating, picturing, comparing, checking, and
choosing so that he never has to, then put a usable deck in front of him
quickly and improve it from what he says.

His own test, for every slide: "can I explain why a slide matters, what you
recommend and what you need from me without asking you to translate it? If
not, keep working on the explanation."

## What must hold on every deck

- **A good deck now beats a perfect deck later.** He is blocked until it
  arrives. Build the smallest deck that answers the ask from the evidence that
  already exists, send it, and improve it from his feedback. "If you make this
  thing be 'build the perfect deck' and I wait for fucking an hour, you just
  actually made everything way fucking worse."
- **Every slide reads cold.** Name each thing by what it is, in full, on the
  slide where it appears: the platform, the campaign, the screen, the feature,
  the group of players. No issue numbers without their plain name, no row or
  section numbers, no handles you coined while working, no "the X" that points
  to another slide, plan, or conversation.
- **Show the thing.** Put the real screen, ad, listing, chart of real data,
  diagram, or a labeled mock on the slide. A sentence that describes a picture
  is a failure: "You should never use words to describe an image. You should
  always put the image."
- **One point per slide.** When a slide needs a paragraph, a packed table, or a
  grid of cards, it is several slides. Split; never shrink the text to fit.
  "I'd rather flip through 200 clear slides than decode 20 dense ones."
- **A clear flow with section dividers.** The answer comes first. The rest is
  grouped into parts by the question each part answers, and every part opens
  with a divider slide. Read only the headlines, in order, and they tell the
  whole story.
- **Compared to what.** Every number carries its unit, period, population, and
  the thing it is compared with. Every proposal shows today beside it.
- **Only what he asked for, and only what he doesn't already know.** No process
  slides, status he didn't ask for, recaps of his own material, or invented
  questions. Nothing important left out either.
- **Only checked, current facts.** Look things up yourself instead of writing
  "needs checking". Label every mock, concept, estimate, and hypothetical.
- **Say what you need from him.** A slide that asks for something says so and
  says what kind of thing it is: needs a yes, a proposal, shipped, FYI, a
  hypothetical. Finish the analysis before you ask.
- **Deliver a Google Slides deck in the Poker Skill Cobalt template**, editable
  by everyone at fun.country. Never a Google Doc, an HTML page, a Claude
  artifact, or a PR when he is the one reviewing it.
- **A clean cold reader reads the draft once before he does.** Rendering and
  text measurement catch layout. Only a reader without your context catches a
  slide that cannot be read cold, and that is the failure that keeps coming
  back. Fix what it finds and send; do not loop.

## The reader

Amir is the founder and CEO of a small company and an engineering-minded
executive. He is a visual thinker: "Without those visuals I have a hard time
really picturing it in my mind." He uses his field's words (paywall, cohort,
conversion, attribution, trial) and should never have them defined. He does not
think in marketer speak ("offer", "lever", "lane", "asks") or in an agent's
working labels. He rounds numbers and speaks in hyperbole for emphasis; do not
make your job verifying him. He needs decisions, not proof: "I need useful
decisions not proof. If everything is unproven my tiny company dies because
we're paralyzed."

A slide he has to decode costs more than the minute it takes. He loses the
thread of the deck ("which causes me to lose the forest"), he stops trusting
the rest of it, and the deck comes back for another round.

## Why decks fail him

Nearly every failure has one cause: the agent wrote from inside its own head
instead of from his side of the screen. It used the labels it coined while
working, followed the order it did the work in, referred to the thing it just
looked at, described a screen it had open, and packed everything it found onto
the slide because all of it felt important. He has none of that context. Write
every slide for a person who walks in from other work, opens only that slide,
and has not seen anything you saw.

## Speed is part of the job

He asks for a deck because he needs to understand or decide something now, and
he waits for it. Every round you run before he sees it is time he spends
blocked, and his reaction to a good draft teaches you more than another round
of your own review. Aim for the first version he can read cold and act on, not
the best version you could make.

That reaches every choice that adds time:

- **Size:** the smallest deck that answers his question. Add a slide only when
  it carries a point he needs.
- **Evidence:** build from the study, data, captures, and mocks that already
  exist. Pull new data, capture new screens, or generate new mocks only when a
  slide cannot be made truthfully without them.
- **Review:** one render-and-look pass and one cold read of the draft, one pass
  of fixes, then send. Say in your message what you know is still rough
  instead of holding the deck back.
- **Long decks:** when the whole deck will take a long time, send the front
  (cover, short version, first part) as soon as it reads cold, and build the
  rest behind it.
- **Revisions:** change only what he named and what his decisions change, and
  send it back as fast as he can read it.

The question to keep asking: is what I am about to do worth making him wait
for?

## How to look at a slide

Ask these as him, one slide at a time. They are a way of seeing, not a gate to
pass.

1. **Can I read this with nothing else open and nothing remembered?** Every
   name, number, and term resolves on this slide.
2. **Do I get the point in five seconds?** The headline states it and one
   picture shows it.
3. **Am I looking at the thing, or at words about it?**
4. **Compared to what?** The unit, the period, the baseline, today.
5. **What do you need from me, and is your part done?**
6. **Is it true today, and is it something I don't already know?**

A deck works when every slide answers all six and the headlines, read in order,
make one argument.

## The flow

- **Open with the answer.** After the cover, a short version: the answer, the
  recommendation, or his goals as you read them ("correct it if we read you
  wrong"), simple enough to act on. "It should be really fucking simple."
  When the deck has parts, the short version lists each part's one-sentence
  answer, in divider order.
- **Group the rest by the question each part answers, and open every part with
  a divider slide**: its number, its name in plain words, one sentence on what
  the part answers, and the slides inside it with their page numbers. A long
  run of content slides reads as one wall: he loses track of which part he is
  in and can't jump to the part he wants. When a part grows long enough that he
  would lose the thread inside it, split it into subsections with their own
  dividers. The cover's contents point at the dividers.
- **Order for the reader, not for the work.** His worry or the answer first.
  Orientation before detail: a picture to hold in mind (the journey at a
  glance, a timeline, a loop) before the deep dive. Cause before effect.
  Evidence before the proposal. A data section follows the story's order: show
  a drop only after the context that explains it, and mark the cause on the
  chart.
- **Chain the headlines.** Each headline should bridge from the one before it.
  When a slide moves, retitle it.
- **Keep the main story short.** Deep dives, superseded drafts, sources, and
  any feedback log go to the back or to an appendix deck. A new research
  question, or a plan that would be buried inside another deck, gets its own
  deck, linked from both.
- **Weave a cross-cutting topic into every part it touches** instead of
  tacking it on as one slide.

## The slide

- **Kicker.** The small capitals above the headline are a label: the section,
  plus the slide's status when it asks for something ("Needs your yes",
  "Proposed", "Concept", "Shipped", "FYI"). Readers skip it, so the headline
  must make sense without it.
- **Headline.** One full sentence on one line that states the takeaway,
  usually with its number: "The rate fell because the dormant pool grew faster
  than returns." Not a topic, a slogan, a clever contrast, a hypothesis, or
  "Keep X". On a growth or opportunity deck, lead with the upside, not a
  downside.
- **One visual, chosen for what the slide means.** Pick the form that carries
  the meaning:
  - the real screen, or a strip of real screens in journey order;
  - today beside the proposal, the proposal made as a GPT Image edit of the
    real capture with only the change, labeled as a mock;
  - a flow drawn in three steps: the journey at a glance, then the journey in
    detail, then one slide per moment with its real screens;
  - a chart of real data with its baseline or goal line;
  - one big number when one fact decides the question;
  - a plain native table for exact values, or for a few things across a few
    attributes, with Poker Skill in the first column;
  - a native, editable diagram for a loop or a mechanism;
  - an illustration for a strategic idea.

  Vary the form across unlike ideas; a run of identical box layouts makes his
  "brain glaze over". Repeat a layout only for a genuinely parallel series.
  When something changes, make the change visible at a glance: the new part
  large and colored, the unchanged parts dimmed.
- **Words.** Short lines that each make sense alone, written as sentences, in
  his vocabulary. Plain is not short: the fixes he accepts often add words,
  such as a full sentence each for today, after the change, why it matters,
  who does it, and when.
- **Speaker notes.** Sources, definitions, method, and caveats go in the notes,
  plus one sources slide at the back. Nothing he needs in order to understand
  the slide may live only in the notes.
- **Off the slide.** Owners, specs, timelines, and other project management;
  build status on a future-facing deck; recaps of his own spreadsheet or plan;
  questions he didn't ask; caveat footers, hedges, and nags; bugs older than
  two weeks or without a link to their issue; anything framed around the past
  when today is the evidence ("stop talking about the spring").

## Calibrate to the audience

The rules above hold for every reader; density, length, and verdicts change.
An advisor or investor deck is the sparsest, with a short main story and an
appendix deck. A team deck uses his words and his priorities. A decision deck
puts one decision on each slide, with its status and economics. A research
deck is complete, one case per slide, with findings and few verdicts. A deck
that orients him on something new stays short and describes before it
prescribes. Read [references/deck-shapes.md](references/deck-shapes.md) when
the deck is for someone other than Amir himself, or when its type is new to
you.

## Workflow

1. **Find the question.** Read his ask word for word and name the reader, what
   the deck is for, and the decision or understanding it should produce. Read
   what the deck needs: the study's own summary and recommendation first, then
   the evidence behind the slides you will make, and any feedback log for this
   deck. If the ask is genuinely ambiguous, ask one short question; otherwise
   proceed.
2. **Use the evidence that exists.** Take numbers with their baselines, real
   screens, and mocks from the study, the research, and the PRs that built the
   feature. Check a status claim against the live system when it is
   time-sensitive and a slide depends on it. Make a new mock only when a slide
   needs one that does not exist, with `$gpt-image` as an edit of a real
   capture, and for Poker Skill follow the AI mock style guide at
   `/Users/aelaguiz/workspace/psbrain/knowledge/ai-mock-style-guide.md`.
3. **Write the headline outline.** The cover, the short version, the parts with
   their dividers, and one headline per slide. Read only the headlines, in
   order, and fix them so they tell the story. Make each content slide's
   headline something that slide can prove on its own. On a deck he is
   co-authoring, build the front well and leave later slides as titled
   placeholders until he has settled it.
4. **Make each slide.** Choose its visual by the meaning, write its words, and
   put sources in its notes. Run `$readable-reports` on the words. When a slide
   is hard to get right, compare it with the pairs in
   [references/examples.md](references/examples.md).
5. **Build it on the Cobalt template.** Read
   [references/cobalt-google-slides.md](references/cobalt-google-slides.md)
   before the first build and whenever a build fails.
6. **Render and look, once.** Render the slides and read each one at full
   size. Then look at the whole deck as a contact sheet: a wall of slides with
   no divider, the same layout repeated across unlike ideas, a missing answer
   at the front. Fix what you see.
7. **Cold read.** Read `../_shared/agent-orchestration-policy.md`, then start a
   clean reviewer with none of your context, on your own model unless he named
   another. Apply `$prompt-authoring` to its brief. Give it his ask verbatim and
   the rendered slide images, nothing else: not your outline, your notes, or
   your view of what the slides mean. Ask it to read each slide alone, as a
   busy founder seeing it for the first time, and report for each one what it
   thinks the slide says, every word, name, or number it could not resolve from
   that slide, what it had to guess, and what it needed to see. Its misreadings
   are the evidence. Fix every slide it could not read cold, then send. Do not
   run a second cold read before he has seen the deck.
8. **Deliver.** Send the link with one or two sentences: what the deck answers,
   what you need from him, and anything you know is still rough. Keep the
   deck's source (builder or spec, images, a feedback log file) next to the
   work it presents.

## When he gives feedback

- If he asks a question or wants to talk it through, answer in the
  conversation before touching the deck. For anything more than a small fix,
  say back what you heard before editing.
- Change what he named. A decision he makes changes every slide that states
  it; a stylistic note applies only to the slide he named.
- Read the live deck before every rebuild. Never overwrite or delete his
  edits, his slides, or slides you didn't make. Keep his order.
- Rebuild only the slides that changed, look at their renders, and send them
  back fast. His next read is the review.
- Keep the feedback log as a file: each round, newest first, with his words,
  your reading of them, and what changed. If the deck carries a log slide, it
  is the last slide.
- When a correction should change how every future deck is built, teach it
  into this skill as an intuition with examples, using `$skill-authoring`.

## Output

- A Google Slides link, sent as soon as the draft reads cold: Cobalt
  template, editable by everyone at fun.country, rendered and cold-read once.
- One or two sentences on what the deck answers and what you need from him.
- The builder or spec, images, and feedback log kept with the work.
