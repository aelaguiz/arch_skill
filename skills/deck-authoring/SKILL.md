# Deck Authoring

Amir opens a deck in the middle of other work, jumps to whatever slide catches
his eye, and reads it cold, often about work he did not watch. From each slide
he keeps one thing, and the headline tells him what it is: "The heading sets
the tone for everything else." Your job is to decide what that one thing is
for every slide, say it simply in his words, and show it, so that he never
has to decode, sort, or picture anything himself. Get the deck to him fast; he is
usually waiting on it.

His test for every slide: "can I explain why a slide matters, what you
recommend and what you need from me without asking you to translate it? If
not, keep working on the explanation."

## The five rules for every slide

1. **The headline is the one thing he should take away, said the way he would
   say it.** "The heading should be one thing that you should take away." If
   he read only that line, he would know the point and could say it back
   without working anything out. He reads everything under the headline
   through it, so a headline with two findings makes him choose what the
   slide is about, and one he has to decode (a negative to flip, two numbers
   to compare, a share of a share) stops him. A second finding is a second
   slide.
2. **A slide that tells him something new holds one thing.** "If it needs to
   be two things then it's two slides." A slide he reads every day in the
   same form, like the KPI scorecard, can hold many numbers, because he
   already knows where to look.
3. **Under the headline goes the picture, not a paragraph.** At most one short
   line says what the picture counts, for whom, and over which days. A word
   that explains a number sits beside that number, in a few words.
4. **His words, not yours.** Name everything the way he names it, from PS
   Brain's word list and glossary or his own messages. When neither has a
   name, say what the user does. Never coin a label, and never fix one by
   defining it: "don't invent words use my words not yours".
5. **A headline that does not fit on one line at the template's 22 points
   (about 65 characters) holds more than one thing.** Cut it to the one
   thing by saying less, never by squeezing more in: a negative, a symbol or
   a stack of qualifiers makes a headline shorter and harder. Never shrink
   the type.

Real headlines from his morning decks, and what to write instead (the first
three are the versions he approved):

| Instead of this | Write this |
| --- | --- |
| 78 in 100 new players never return after day one; 8 in 100 ever hit the energy limit | Most new users leave before the energy gate can sell them Plus |
| AEO: Friday's answers that read our page named us 17 times in 19; the others, never | AI assistants name us only when they read one of our pages |
| Half of first-month money comes in 5 minutes; most lost free weeks go on day one | Two slides: "Nearly all of D30 LTV comes from D0 conversions" and "Most iOS trials that get cancelled are cancelled on D0" |
| No build fell below 97% of days with no failed step Tuesday | Under 3% of users' days hit a failed step on any build Tuesday |
| International iOS installs will bring 34% of what new users pay, vs 20% | International iOS users now bring a third of new users' revenue (up from a fifth goes under it) |
| Three running experiments have a result; two of them convert fewer | The energy gate and Home first experiments cost us conversions |

**Look at the pictures before your first headline in a session.**
[references/bad-and-good.md](references/bad-and-good.md) holds each bad slide
and its good version as images. Open every image with your image-viewing
tool; the headlines alone do not show what a slide with one thing on it looks
like.

## Also true of every deck

- **Fast.** A good deck now beats a perfect one later. Build the smallest deck
  that answers the ask from evidence that already exists, send it, and improve
  it from what he says: "If you make this thing be 'build the perfect deck'
  and I wait for fucking an hour, you just actually made everything way
  fucking worse."
- **Every slide reads cold.** Each thing is named in full on the slide where
  it appears. No issue numbers without their plain name, no row or section
  numbers, no "the X" that points to another slide, plan, or conversation.
- **Show the thing:** the real screen, ad, listing, chart of real data,
  diagram, or a labeled mock. "You should never use words to describe an
  image. You should always put the image."
- **The answer first, then parts opened by section dividers.** The headlines,
  read alone in order, tell the whole story.
- **Every slide is numbered,** cover and dividers included, so he can say
  "slide 14".
- **Compared to what.** Every number carries its unit, period, population, and
  comparison, in the picture or the line under the headline. Every proposal
  shows today beside it.
- **Only what he asked for and doesn't already know,** with nothing important
  left out.
- **Only checked, current facts.** Look things up instead of writing "needs
  checking". Label every mock, estimate, and hypothetical.
- **Say what you need from him.** The kicker says what kind of slide it is:
  needs a yes, proposed, shipped, FYI.
- **A Google Slides deck in the Poker Skill Cobalt template,** editable by
  everyone at fun.country. Never a Google Doc, an HTML page, a Claude
  artifact, or a PR when he is the one reviewing it.

## The headline

He reads it first, reads the slide through it, and repeats it. So it carries
the one thing he should take away, as one sentence about one subject, on one
line at full size, said the way he would say it. Not a topic, a slogan, a
clever contrast, or a hypothesis. On a growth deck, lead with the upside.

Write it as you would tell him across the table: the plain sentence a person
says out loud, about what is true and what it means for him, never how you
measured it. Amir, October 14, after a morning of decks: "It's just really
fucking confusingly long titles when it could say simple things." He reads
twenty decks between other work and acts on what he takes from each line, so
a headline he has to decode costs him a stop and can hide the news or say
its opposite. That morning, "No user at the energy gate told us why they
didn't convert: 0 of 1,288" hid that the app skips the question inside a
lesson, and "No paywall after D0 is my lane's surest way to keep users
active" reads as if no paywall works.

The failures come from writing inside your own head and from fitting a full
finding into one line: you found many true things, each feels important, and
they all go in, squeezed. They take a few recurring shapes:

- **Two findings joined by a semicolon or "and".** "78 in 100 new players
  never return after day one; 8 in 100 ever hit the energy limit." The
  slide's real point sat at the end of its third paragraph. Write: "Most new
  users leave before the energy gate can sell them Plus."
- **A negative where the finding is.** Instead of "Sizes that changed after
  the decks were built change no decision", write "Every call stands after
  this morning's late changes". Say what is true; he should never have to
  flip a "no", "none", "nothing" or "neither" to find it. When the news
  really is an absence, say the state it leaves: instead of "No user on
  either 2.1.50 build crashed on its first full day", write "2.1.50 had a
  crash-free first full day on iOS and Android"; instead of "2.1.50 reopened
  none of its 21 fixes on either store", write "All 21 fixes in 2.1.50 are
  holding on iOS and Android". When the answer is not known yet, the
  headline says what we know so far and the slide says what would settle it.
- **How you measured it, instead of what it means.** Instead of "Even our
  cheapest ad costs $25 per trial start, worth $8.89 in 30 days", write
  "Every ad costs about three times what its trial starts bring in".
  A threshold cleared, a share of a share, two raw numbers side by side: do
  the arithmetic for him and say the result; the exact numbers go under the
  headline.
- **The finding with every qualifier attached.** Instead of "The App Review
  paywall fallback costs no checkouts we can see yet", write "Users shown the
  App Review paywall by mistake still checked out"; how many, and that it
  is early, go on the line under it.
- **A phrase only you would use.** "Of the 20 fixes waiting for your merge,
  two cost this week's goals" makes him unpack an odd verb. Write: "Two fixes
  waiting for your merge matter this week." Likewise "Money comes in five
  minutes" hides who did what: "Half of D30 LTV comes from users who convert
  in their first five minutes."
- **A parallel with the second verb dropped.** "Android pays at the limit,
  iPhone holds free." He has to rebuild the second half from the first. Say
  what the comparison means: "AI assistants name us only when they read one
  of our pages", with the 17 of 19 and the 0 of 8 on the slide as evidence.

A comparison on one measure is one point when the comparison is the news:
"More users come back the day after a lesson: 75% against 61%." Say it the
way he would ("twice as often", "a third, up from a fifth") when the raw
numbers would make him do the math. A number belongs in the headline when it
is the point; its counts, baseline, and period go on the slide.

Before you keep a headline, say it out loud as you would to him. If you
would say it differently, write what you would say. If he would have to stop
to work out what it means, it is not done.

When the headline will not fit on one line at 22 points, it holds more than
one thing, or says one thing the hard way. Say less: move the second thing
to its own slide and the qualifiers and numbers onto the slide. Never squeeze
it with a negative, a symbol or a noun stack, never shrink the type, and
never let a builder set it smaller.

## Under the headline

His eye goes from the headline to the picture, and anything between them
competes with the headline. So under it goes the picture, with at most one
short line saying what it counts, for whom, and over which days ("New users
who first opened Sep 15 to Oct 2"). A word that explains a number sits beside
that number. An explanation that needs a paragraph is either another point,
so another slide, or method, so speaker notes. To check, cover the headline
and the picture and read what is left: if he needs it to get the point, it is
another slide; if not, it goes to the notes.

## Two kinds of slide

He reads a recurring slide by knowing where to look. The KPI scorecard and
other standing data views keep the same rows, order, units, and form every
day, so a change jumps out, and they can hold many numbers because he already
holds the frame. The headline names the one thing that moved most, or simply
what the slide is. Every other slide tells him something new: a finding, a
proposal, an explanation. A morning report is a daily deck, but almost all of
its slides are new each morning. Ask of each slide: would he have seen this
form and these rows yesterday? If not, it holds one thing.

## His words

The names you give things while you work ("free weeks", "next-day return", "a
level") are yours. He meets them cold, has to translate each one, and the
next deck invents another. Before you write headlines, list the things the
deck names and take each name from PS Brain's glossary
(`/Users/aelaguiz/workspace/psbrain/shared/glossary.md`), his word-list
rulings (`/Users/aelaguiz/workspace/psbrain/projects/report-language/README.md`,
until the word list replaces the glossary), or the words in his own messages.
For example, he says users (players only for people at a poker table), trial,
paywall, energy gate, iOS for the platform, proposal (never "move"), D0, D1,
D7, and D30 for days, and % with the counts behind it. The sources are the
authority; this is only a sample.

When no source has a name, say what the user does ("users who cancel their
trial on D0"). A metric always carries its horizon and unit, and keeps the
same unit on every slide. When he trips on a word, replace it on every slide;
defining it keeps the word, and he trips on it again.

## The flow

- **Open with the answer.** After the cover, a short version: the answer, the
  recommendation, or his goals as you read them ("correct it if we read you
  wrong"), simple enough to act on. When he is getting his bearings, the
  answer is the picture of the whole and recommendations wait; when he is
  running the business from the deck, the proposals come first. When the
  deck has parts, the short version lists each part's one-sentence answer,
  in divider order: what that part achieves, never a count of what it holds
  ("two sets of actions").
- **Group the rest by the question each part answers, and open every part with
  a divider:** its number, its plain name, one sentence on what it answers,
  and its slides with page numbers. Split a long part into subsections with
  their own dividers. The cover's contents point at the dividers.
- **Order for the reader, not for the work.** His worry or the answer first,
  a simple picture before the deep dive, a complicated system built up a
  layer per slide ("you need to ease me into the architecture"), cause before
  effect, evidence before the proposal.
- **Chain the headlines** by the order of the ideas, never by a word an
  earlier slide set up: he lands on slide 14 without slide 13, so "the same
  gap" names nothing. A total in the short version or on a divider
  reconciles with the figures in the headlines beneath it, in the same units
  and without counting anything twice, with any rounding said.
- **Keep the main story short.** Deep dives, superseded drafts, and sources go
  to the back or an appendix deck; a new research question gets its own deck.

## The slide

- **Kicker:** the section, plus the status when the slide asks for something
  ("Needs your yes", "Proposed", "Concept", "Shipped", "FYI"). Readers skip
  it, so the headline stands without it.
- **One picture, chosen for what the slide means:** the real screen or a strip
  of them in journey order; today beside the proposal, made as a GPT Image
  edit of the real capture and labeled as a mock; a chart of real data with
  its baseline; one big number when one fact decides the question; a plain
  native table for exact values; a native diagram for a loop or mechanism; an
  illustration for a strategic idea. Vary the form across unlike ideas; a run
  of identical layouts makes his "brain glaze over". When something changes,
  make the change large and colored and dim the rest.
- **Words:** short lines that each make sense alone, as sentences. Plain is
  not short: a change slide he accepted had one sentence each for today,
  after, why, who, and when, each beside its label.
- **Speaker notes:** sources, definitions, method, and caveats, plus one
  sources slide at the back. Nothing he needs for the point lives only there.
- **Off the slide:** owners, specs, timelines, build status on a
  future-facing deck, recaps of his own material, questions he didn't ask,
  caveat footers and hedges, stale bugs, and the past when today is the
  evidence.

Density, length, and verdicts change with the reader and the deck's job. Read
[references/deck-shapes.md](references/deck-shapes.md) when the deck is for
someone other than Amir, is meant to orient him on something new, or is a
type new to you.

## Workflow

1. **Find the question.** Read his ask word for word: the reader, what the
   deck is for, and the decision or understanding it should produce. Read the
   study's summary first, then the evidence behind your slides and any
   feedback log for this deck. Ask one short question only if the ask is
   genuinely ambiguous.
2. **Use the evidence that exists:** numbers with their baselines, real
   screens, and mocks from the study and the PRs that built the feature.
   Check a time-sensitive status claim against the live system. Make a new
   mock only when a slide needs one, with `$gpt-image` as an edit of a real
   capture, following
   `/Users/aelaguiz/workspace/psbrain/knowledge/ai-mock-style-guide.md`.
3. **Write the headline outline.** If you have not opened the images in
   [references/bad-and-good.md](references/bad-and-good.md) this session, do
   it now. List the things the deck names and settle each name. Then write the
   cover, the short version, the parts with their dividers, and one headline
   per slide: the one thing he takes away from that slide, in his words, on
   one line. A slide with two things becomes two slides here, before anything
   is built. Read the headlines alone, in order, aloud as you would say them
   to him, and fix them until they tell the story in plain sentences he would
   use. Make each content slide's headline something that slide can prove on
   its own. On a deck he is co-authoring, build the front from his words and
   leave later slides as titled placeholders until he settles it.
4. **Make each slide.** Pick its picture, write the one line under the
   headline, and put sources in the notes. Use `$readable-reports` for prose:
   speaker notes, the message that sends the deck, and any sentences a slide
   needs. Its rule that a number arrives with its comparison is met by the
   picture and the line under the headline, never by packing the headline.
   When a slide is hard to get right, read
   [references/examples.md](references/examples.md).
5. **Build it on the Cobalt template.** Read
   [references/cobalt-google-slides.md](references/cobalt-google-slides.md)
   before the first build and whenever a build fails.
6. **Render and look, once.** Read each slide at full size. A headline that
   wraps, or that a builder set below 22 points, holds two things: cut it. A
   block of text under the headline or a number that runs past a short line
   is a paragraph: make it another slide or move it to the notes. Then look
   at the deck as a contact sheet for a wall with no dividers, one layout
   repeated across unlike ideas, or a missing answer at the front.
7. **Cold read, once.** Read `../_shared/agent-orchestration-policy.md`, then
   start a clean reviewer with none of your context, on your own model unless
   he named another, and apply `$prompt-authoring` to its brief. Give it his
   ask verbatim and the rendered slide images, nothing else. Ask it to read
   each slide alone, as a busy founder seeing it for the first time, and
   answer four questions per slide:
   1. In one sentence, what is this slide's one point?
   2. Does the headline say that point, and only that point, as plainly as
      your sentence?
   3. Which words, names, numbers or sentences would the founder of this app
      not say himself, or could not resolve from this slide alone?
   4. What text could go without losing the point, including any line that
      narrates how the deck was made?

   Its answers are the places a reader had to work, not a verdict; it is more
   patient than he is and does not know his product. Where its one-sentence
   point is plainer than your headline, that sentence is usually the better
   headline. Fix each place by cutting, splitting the slide, or replacing a
   word with his. Never fix one
   by adding a definition, a count, or a caveat: the slide gets denser, and he
   still stops at the first word he would not say himself. Whether a sentence
   is true about the app is yours to check. Then send; no second cold read
   before he has seen the deck.
8. **Deliver.** Send the link with one or two sentences: what the deck
   answers, what you need from him, and anything still rough. Keep the
   builder or spec, images, and a feedback log file next to the work.

## When he gives feedback

- He usually dictates while he flips through: a live read, not a list of
  edits. Words he reads off a slide are yours coming back, not his decision.
  He reports the first thing that stopped him and may not have read the rest.
  Dictation garbles names, drops a "not", and drifts his slide numbers, so
  match by sense and mark your guesses when you say it back.
- If he asks a question, answer it before touching the deck. For more than a
  small fix, say back what you heard before editing.
- Change what he named, as far as it reaches. A decision he makes changes
  every slide that states it. A slide he could not read usually shows a habit
  of yours (a made-up word, two findings in one headline, a result with no
  setup): fix it on every slide. A note about taste applies only to its slide.
- Read the live deck before every rebuild. Never overwrite or delete his
  edits or slides you didn't make, and keep his order. Rebuild only what
  changed and send it back fast.
- Keep the feedback log as a file, newest round first: his words, your
  reading, what changed.
- When a correction should change every future deck, teach it into this skill
  as an intuition with examples, using `$skill-authoring`.
